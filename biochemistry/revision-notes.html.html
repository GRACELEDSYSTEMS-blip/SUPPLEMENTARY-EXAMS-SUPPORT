FOUNDATIONS

1. Foundations of Metabolism

Big picture

Metabolism is an ordered network of enzyme-catalysed pathways. Catabolic pathways are generally oxidative and exergonic and capture energy in ATP/reduced coenzymes; anabolic pathways are generally reductive and endergonic and use that energy to build molecules.

Pathway patterns

Linear pathways proceed stepwise without regenerating the starting molecule (glycolysis). Cyclic pathways regenerate a key intermediate (TCA cycle regenerates oxaloacetate). Spiral pathways repeat a reaction sequence while changing chain length by a fixed unit (β-oxidation removes 2-carbon units).

Free energy

ΔG predicts thermodynamic favourability, not reaction speed. At equilibrium ΔG = 0 and there is no net concentration change. Enzymes lower activation energy and speed approach to equilibrium; they do not change ΔG or the equilibrium position.

Energy coupling

ATP, GTP, NADH and FADH₂ link energy-producing and energy-requiring processes. Coupling works when the favourable and unfavourable reactions are mechanistically linked.

⚠ Exam traps
  • Do not confuse thermodynamics (ΔG) with kinetics (rate).
  • A positive ΔG means the forward reaction is unfavourable under the stated conditions.
  • A metabolic block tends to cause upstream accumulation and downstream depletion/diversion.
🧠 Before you leave this topic
  • Metabolism is an ordered network of enzyme-catalysed pathways. Catabolic pathways are generally oxidative and exergonic and capture energy in ATP/reduced coenzymes; anabolic pathways are generally reductive and endergonic and use that energy to build molecules.
  • ATP, GTP, NADH and FADH₂ link energy-producing and energy-requiring processes. Coupling works when the favourable and unfavourable reactions are mechanistically linked.
ENZYMES

2. Enzymes, Kinetics & Inhibition

Core terms

Apoenzyme = protein portion alone. Cofactor = required non-protein component. Holoenzyme = active apoenzyme + required cofactor. Organic cofactors are commonly called coenzymes; many are vitamin-derived.

Michaelis–Menten essentials

Km is the substrate concentration at which v = Vmax/2. A lower Km generally reflects higher apparent substrate affinity. Vmax is approached when enzyme active sites are saturated.

Competitive inhibition

Inhibitor competes with substrate for the active site. Apparent Km increases; Vmax is unchanged. High substrate can overcome reversible competitive inhibition.

Pure non-competitive inhibition

Inhibitor binds E and ES equally at a site distinct from the active site. Vmax decreases; Km remains unchanged. Increasing substrate cannot restore the lost Vmax.

Uncompetitive inhibition

Inhibitor binds only the ES complex. Both apparent Km and Vmax decrease.

Allostery & feedback

Allosteric enzymes often show cooperative, sigmoidal kinetics. Feedback inhibition commonly lets an end product inhibit an early committed regulatory enzyme, preventing wasteful overproduction.

Clinical value

Isoenzymes catalyse the same overall reaction but differ in structure/tissue distribution; isoenzyme patterns can help localise tissue injury.

⚠ Exam traps
  • Enzymes change activation energy, NOT ΔG or equilibrium.
  • At [S] = Km, velocity is Vmax/2 — not Vmax.
  • Do not assume every reversible inhibitor raises Km.
  • Fluoride inhibits enolase; it is not a classic irreversible covalent inhibitor.
🧠 Before you leave this topic
  • Apoenzyme = protein portion alone. Cofactor = required non-protein component. Holoenzyme = active apoenzyme + required cofactor. Organic cofactors are commonly called coenzymes; many are vitamin-derived.
  • Isoenzymes catalyse the same overall reaction but differ in structure/tissue distribution; isoenzyme patterns can help localise tissue injury.
GLYCOLYSIS

3. Glycolysis

Glucose → G6P → F6P → F1,6BP → triose phosphates → 1,3-BPG → 3-PG → 2-PG → PEP → Pyruvate

Big picture

Glycolysis is a 10-step cytosolic pathway converting glucose to pyruvate. It can produce ATP without oxygen because its ATP-forming reactions are substrate-level phosphorylations.

Irreversible control points

Hexokinase/glucokinase: glucose → glucose-6-phosphate. PFK-1: fructose-6-phosphate → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate (committed/rate-limiting step). Pyruvate kinase: PEP → pyruvate.

PFK-1 regulation

Activated by AMP/ADP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate. Inhibited by high ATP and citrate.

Hexokinase vs glucokinase

Hexokinase has low Km (high apparent affinity) and is inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate. Glucokinase has higher Km/higher capacity, is mainly in liver and pancreatic β-cells, and is not inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate.

Payoff phase

Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase forms NADH. Phosphoglycerate kinase and pyruvate kinase generate ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation.

Anaerobic fate

Lactate dehydrogenase converts pyruvate to lactate while oxidising NADH to NAD⁺. Regenerating NAD⁺ allows glycolysis to continue when oxidative capacity is limited.

RBC connection

Mature RBCs lack mitochondria and depend on glycolysis for ATP. Pyruvate kinase deficiency reduces RBC ATP and can cause chronic haemolytic anaemia.

Rapoport–Luebering shunt

Produces 2,3-BPG in RBCs. Increased 2,3-BPG lowers haemoglobin oxygen affinity and promotes oxygen unloading to tissues.

⚠ Exam traps
  • PFK-1 uses the SECOND ATP invested in glycolysis.
  • Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate activates PFK-1 and inhibits FBPase-1.
  • Glucokinase is NOT inhibited by G6P.
  • Fluoride inhibits enolase.
🧠 Before you leave this topic
  • Glycolysis is a 10-step cytosolic pathway converting glucose to pyruvate. It can produce ATP without oxygen because its ATP-forming reactions are substrate-level phosphorylations.
  • Produces 2,3-BPG in RBCs. Increased 2,3-BPG lowers haemoglobin oxygen affinity and promotes oxygen unloading to tissues.
GLUCONEOGENESIS

4. Gluconeogenesis

Pyruvate --pyruvate carboxylase→ OAA --PEPCK→ PEP … F1,6BP --FBPase-1→ F6P … G6P --G6-phosphatase→ Glucose

Big picture

Gluconeogenesis maintains blood glucose during fasting. It occurs mainly in liver, with kidney contribution especially during prolonged fasting. It is not simply glycolysis in reverse because the three irreversible glycolytic steps require bypasses.

Bypass 1

Pyruvate → oxaloacetate by mitochondrial pyruvate carboxylase (ATP + biotin; activated by acetyl-CoA). Oxaloacetate → PEP by PEP carboxykinase (PEPCK), using GTP.

Bypass 2

Fructose-1,6-bisphosphate → fructose-6-phosphate by fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (FBPase-1).

Bypass 3

Glucose-6-phosphate → free glucose by glucose-6-phosphatase, associated with the ER. Liver and kidney can perform this step; skeletal muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.

Energy cost

From 2 pyruvate to 1 glucose: 4 ATP + 2 GTP + 2 NADH.

Substrates

Alanine → pyruvate; glycerol → glycerol-3-phosphate → DHAP; glucogenic amino acids feed pyruvate/TCA intermediates; odd-chain fatty acids can contribute via propionyl-CoA → succinyl-CoA.

Hormonal/energy regulation

Glucagon promotes hepatic gluconeogenesis through cAMP/PKA; insulin suppresses it. AMP signals low energy and inhibits gluconeogenesis. High acetyl-CoA activates pyruvate carboxylase and inhibits pyruvate dehydrogenase.

Reciprocal control

Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate inhibits FBPase-1 and activates PFK-1, shifting flux toward glycolysis.

⚠ Exam traps
  • Muscle cannot release free glucose because it lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.
  • Acetyl-CoA ACTIVATES pyruvate carboxylase.
  • Even-chain fatty-acid acetyl-CoA does not provide net glucose in animals.
  • High ATP does not automatically inhibit gluconeogenesis; the pathway requires a high-energy hepatic state.
🧠 Before you leave this topic
  • Gluconeogenesis maintains blood glucose during fasting. It occurs mainly in liver, with kidney contribution especially during prolonged fasting. It is not simply glycolysis in reverse because the three irreversible glycolytic steps require bypasses.
  • Fructose-2,6-bisphosphate inhibits FBPase-1 and activates PFK-1, shifting flux toward glycolysis.
FRUCTOSE / GALACTOSE

5. Fructose & Galactose Metabolism

Fructose entry

In liver, fructokinase converts fructose to fructose-1-phosphate. Aldolase B then yields DHAP and glyceraldehyde, allowing entry downstream of PFK-1.

Essential fructosuria

Fructokinase deficiency. Usually benign.

Hereditary fructose intolerance

Aldolase B deficiency → fructose-1-phosphate accumulation. Typical exam clues include symptoms after fructose-containing foods, vomiting and hypoglycaemia.

Galactose entry

Galactokinase forms galactose-1-phosphate. The Leloir pathway uses UDP-galactose/UDP-glucose intermediates to generate glucose-1-phosphate.

Classic galactosaemia

Galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase (GALT) deficiency → galactose-1-phosphate accumulation and tissue toxicity.

Galactokinase deficiency

Promotes galactitol accumulation, particularly in the lens, and is associated with cataracts.

⚠ Exam traps
  • Fructokinase deficiency ≠ hereditary fructose intolerance.
  • HFI = aldolase B deficiency.
  • Classic galactosaemia = GALT deficiency.
  • Galactokinase deficiency classically points toward galactitol/cataracts.
🧠 Before you leave this topic
  • In liver, fructokinase converts fructose to fructose-1-phosphate. Aldolase B then yields DHAP and glyceraldehyde, allowing entry downstream of PFK-1.
  • Promotes galactitol accumulation, particularly in the lens, and is associated with cataracts.
PPP

6. Pentose Phosphate Pathway

Main purposes

The PPP provides NADPH and pentose phosphates. The oxidative phase is irreversible and generates NADPH; the non-oxidative phase reversibly rearranges sugar phosphates.

Oxidative phase

Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) is the key/rate-limiting enzyme. Rising NADP⁺ availability promotes oxidative PPP flux. The immediate pentose product is ribulose-5-phosphate.

Non-oxidative phase

Transketolase and transaldolase interconvert sugar phosphates. Transketolase requires thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP); transaldolase does not.

Ribose need without NADPH

Rapidly proliferating cells can use reversible non-oxidative reactions to generate ribose-5-phosphate from glycolytic intermediates without necessarily producing NADPH.

RBC antioxidant defence

RBCs depend on PPP-derived NADPH. NADPH allows glutathione reductase to regenerate reduced glutathione (GSH), which protects haemoglobin and membranes from reactive oxygen species.

G6PD deficiency

Reduced NADPH → impaired GSH regeneration → vulnerability to oxidant-induced haemolysis.

⚠ Exam traps
  • Only the oxidative phase generates NADPH.
  • G6PD — not transaldolase — is rate-limiting.
  • Transketolase requires TPP.
  • NADPH supports glutathione reductase; G6PD does not directly reduce GSSG.
🧠 Before you leave this topic
  • The PPP provides NADPH and pentose phosphates. The oxidative phase is irreversible and generates NADPH; the non-oxidative phase reversibly rearranges sugar phosphates.
  • Reduced NADPH → impaired GSH regeneration → vulnerability to oxidant-induced haemolysis.
GLYCOGEN

7. Glycogen Metabolism

Synthesis

Glycogenin autoglycosylates a tyrosine residue using UDP-glucose to create a primer. Glycogen synthase extends α-1,4 chains; branching enzyme creates α-1,6 branches.

Breakdown

Glycogen phosphorylase removes glucose residues from α-1,4 linkages. Debranching enzyme handles branch points and has transferase plus α-1,6-glucosidase activities.

Hormonal control

Insulin promotes PP1-mediated dephosphorylation and activates glycogen synthase while favouring inactivation of glycogen phosphorylase. Glucagon stimulates hepatic glycogenolysis through cAMP/PKA; epinephrine promotes glycogenolysis in liver and muscle.

Liver vs muscle

Liver glycogen supports blood glucose. Muscle glycogen is for local energy use because muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.

Key disorders

Von Gierke: glucose-6-phosphatase system deficiency. McArdle: muscle glycogen phosphorylase deficiency → exercise intolerance and poor lactate rise with ischemic exercise. Andersen: branching enzyme deficiency → abnormally long, poorly branched chains.

⚠ Exam traps
  • Glycogen synthase is active when dephosphorylated.
  • Glycogen phosphorylase breaks α-1,4 bonds; debranching enzyme is needed at branches.
  • Muscle glycogen does NOT directly maintain blood glucose.
  • Do not confuse branching-enzyme deficiency (Andersen) with debranching defects.
🧠 Before you leave this topic
  • Glycogenin autoglycosylates a tyrosine residue using UDP-glucose to create a primer. Glycogen synthase extends α-1,4 chains; branching enzyme creates α-1,6 branches.
  • Von Gierke: glucose-6-phosphatase system deficiency. McArdle: muscle glycogen phosphorylase deficiency → exercise intolerance and poor lactate rise with ischemic exercise. Andersen: branching enzyme deficiency → abnormally long, poorly branched chains.
TCA CYCLE

8. TCA / Citric Acid Cycle

Big picture

The TCA cycle is mitochondrial, cyclic and amphibolic. Oxaloacetate is regenerated. It oxidises acetyl-CoA while producing reduced electron carriers that feed oxidative phosphorylation.

Amphibolic role

The cycle participates in both degradation and biosynthesis. Its intermediates can be withdrawn for synthetic pathways and replenished by anaplerotic reactions.

Key integration point

Acetyl-CoA links carbohydrate, fatty-acid and amino-acid metabolism. NADH/FADH₂ generated by oxidative metabolism transfer reducing equivalents to the respiratory chain.

Succinate dehydrogenase

A special enzyme because it functions in the TCA cycle and as ETC Complex II. It oxidises succinate to fumarate through bound FAD and transfers electrons to ubiquinone.

⚠ Exam traps
  • Amphibolic does NOT mean exclusively catabolic.
  • The cycle is cyclic because oxaloacetate is regenerated.
  • Succinate dehydrogenase = TCA enzyme + ETC Complex II.
🧠 Before you leave this topic
  • The TCA cycle is mitochondrial, cyclic and amphibolic. Oxaloacetate is regenerated. It oxidises acetyl-CoA while producing reduced electron carriers that feed oxidative phosphorylation.
  • A special enzyme because it functions in the TCA cycle and as ETC Complex II. It oxidises succinate to fumarate through bound FAD and transfers electrons to ubiquinone.
ETC

9. ETC & Oxidative Phosphorylation

NADH → I → CoQ → III → cytochrome c → IV → O₂ → H₂O\nFADH₂ → II → CoQ → III → cytochrome c → IV → O₂ → H₂O\nH⁺ gradient → ATP synthase → ATP

Location & purpose

The ETC is in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Electron transfer is coupled to proton pumping, creating a proton-motive force that drives ATP synthase.

Complex I

Accepts electrons from NADH through FMN/Fe-S centres, transfers them to ubiquinone and pumps protons.

Complex II

Succinate dehydrogenase. Accepts electrons via FAD/FADH₂ and transfers them to ubiquinone. It does NOT pump protons.

Complex III

Transfers electrons from ubiquinol to cytochrome c through the Q cycle and contributes to proton translocation.

Complex IV

Cytochrome c oxidase accepts electrons from cytochrome c and reduces O₂ to H₂O. Oxygen is the terminal electron acceptor.

Mobile carriers

Ubiquinone (CoQ) carries electrons within the membrane; cytochrome c is a soluble peripheral carrier on the outer surface of the inner membrane.

ATP synthase

F₀ forms the proton channel; F₁ projects into the matrix and catalyses ATP synthesis as protons flow down the electrochemical gradient.

ATP yield

Modern P/O estimates used in the supplied keys: mitochondrial NADH ≈ 2.5 ATP; FADH₂ ≈ 1.5 ATP because FADH₂-derived electrons enter downstream of Complex I.

Shuttle

The malate–aspartate shuttle transfers cytosolic reducing equivalents as mitochondrial NADH and is prominent in tissues such as liver and heart.

⚠ Exam traps
  • Complexes I, III and IV pump protons; Complex II does not.
  • O₂ accepts electrons at Complex IV.
  • Cytochrome c is not the lipid-soluble carrier — CoQ is.
  • FADH₂ yields less ATP because its electrons bypass Complex I.
🧠 Before you leave this topic
  • The ETC is in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Electron transfer is coupled to proton pumping, creating a proton-motive force that drives ATP synthase.
  • The malate–aspartate shuttle transfers cytosolic reducing equivalents as mitochondrial NADH and is prominent in tissues such as liver and heart.
INHIBITORS / UNCOUPLERS

10. ETC Inhibitors & Uncouplers

Rotenone

Inhibits Complex I by blocking electron transfer to ubiquinone.

Malonate

Competitive inhibitor of succinate dehydrogenase (Complex II) at the succinate-binding site.

Antimycin A

Inhibits Complex III.

Cyanide

Inhibits Complex IV at the heme-copper oxygen-reduction centre, preventing oxygen reduction and rapidly stopping oxidative phosphorylation.

Oligomycin

Blocks the F₀ proton channel of ATP synthase. Proton re-entry falls, the gradient builds, and electron transport/oxygen consumption slow secondarily.

2,4-DNP

A protonophore/uncoupler. Dissipates the proton gradient without directly blocking a respiratory complex. Electron transport and oxygen consumption may continue or increase while ATP synthesis falls and heat production rises.

Physiological uncoupling

Brown adipose tissue uses UCP1 to dissipate the proton gradient as heat.

⚠ Exam traps
  • Uncoupler ≠ ETC-complex inhibitor.
  • DNP does NOT directly inhibit Complex I.
  • Oligomycin is an ATP-synthase inhibitor, not an uncoupler.
  • Cyanide blocks Complex IV; rotenone I; antimycin A III.
🧠 Before you leave this topic
  • Inhibits Complex I by blocking electron transfer to ubiquinone.
  • Brown adipose tissue uses UCP1 to dissipate the proton gradient as heat.
BETA-OXIDATION

11. β-Oxidation of Fatty Acids

Acyl-CoA → Oxidation (FADH₂) → Hydration → Oxidation (NADH) → Thiolysis → Acetyl-CoA + shortened acyl-CoA

Big picture

β-Oxidation is a mitochondrial spiral pathway that repeatedly shortens fatty acyl-CoA by two carbons, generating acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH₂.

Activation & entry

Fatty acids are activated to fatty acyl-CoA. Long-chain fatty acyl groups require the carnitine shuttle: CPT-I forms acylcarnitine, the translocase crosses the inner membrane, and CPT-II reforms acyl-CoA in the matrix.

Four recurring reactions

1) Oxidation by acyl-CoA dehydrogenase → trans-Δ²-enoyl-CoA + FADH₂. 2) Hydration by enoyl-CoA hydratase. 3) Oxidation by 3-hydroxyacyl-CoA dehydrogenase → NADH. 4) Thiolysis by thiolase → acetyl-CoA + acyl-CoA shortened by 2 carbons.

Odd-chain fatty acids

The final 3-carbon fragment is propionyl-CoA. It is converted through methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA; methylmalonyl-CoA mutase requires adenosylcobalamin (vitamin B12).

Fasting integration

Fasting increases fatty-acid delivery to liver. β-Oxidation raises acetyl-CoA and NADH, favouring glucose conservation and gluconeogenesis.

⚠ Exam traps
  • CPT-I is part of the carnitine shuttle, not one of the four β-oxidation spiral reactions.
  • Each ordinary turn produces 1 FADH₂ and 1 NADH.
  • Most turns release one acetyl-CoA; final cleavage of a 4-carbon intermediate yields two.
  • B12 is important for odd-chain fatty-acid metabolism via methylmalonyl-CoA mutase.
🧠 Before you leave this topic
  • β-Oxidation is a mitochondrial spiral pathway that repeatedly shortens fatty acyl-CoA by two carbons, generating acetyl-CoA, NADH and FADH₂.
  • Fasting increases fatty-acid delivery to liver. β-Oxidation raises acetyl-CoA and NADH, favouring glucose conservation and gluconeogenesis.
