⚡ Last-Minute Mode

Do not cram these blindly. Make sure you can explain each bold idea in your own words.

Bonding decides shape.
  • Alkane carbon: sp³, tetrahedral.
  • Alkene carbon: sp², trigonal planar.
  • Alkyne carbon: sp, linear.
Unsaturation changes reactivity.

π bonds are more exposed than σ bonds, so alkenes and alkynes readily undergo addition reactions.

Restricted rotation matters.

A C=C bond cannot freely rotate. This explains cis/trans isomerism in suitable alkenes.

Know oxidation pathways.

1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid
2° alcohol → ketone

SN2 = one step.

Bond-making and bond-breaking occur together. Steric hindrance slows attack; inversion occurs at the reacting stereocentre.

E2 = one step.

A base removes a β-H while the leaving group leaves and the double bond forms simultaneously.

Aromaticity gives special stability.

Benzene tends to preserve aromaticity, so its characteristic reactions are substitutions.

Carbonyl carbon is electrophilic.

The C=O bond is polar. Nucleophiles attack the carbonyl carbon in many reactions.

Carboxylic acids form carboxylates.

They are weak acids; their conjugate bases are resonance stabilized.

Amines are basic.

The nitrogen lone pair can accept H⁺. Reduced lone-pair availability generally lowers basicity.

High-yield reaction chain

Alkane → Alkyl halide → Alcohol ⇄ Alkene
1° Alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid   |   2° Alcohol → Ketone
Carboxylic acid + Alcohol ⇌ Ester + H₂O
TOPIC 1

Foundations + Alkanes

What the exam is really testing

  • General formulae, carbon hybridisation and geometry.
  • Primary, secondary and tertiary carbon atoms.
  • IUPAC naming and structural isomerism.
  • Physical-property trends with chain length and branching.
  • Combustion, halogenation, cracking and reforming.
Digest it: Alkanes contain only C–C and C–H σ bonds. They are relatively non-polar and hydrophobic. Their lack of an exposed π bond helps explain why they are less reactive than alkenes and alkynes.

Branching: for isomeric alkanes, greater branching generally reduces surface contact and tends to lower boiling point.

TOPIC 2

Alkenes

An alkene contains a carbon–carbon double bond. Each double-bond carbon is sp² hybridised and approximately trigonal planar.

Digest it: A double bond contains one σ bond and one π bond. The π bond prevents free rotation and is the more accessible part during many addition reactions.

Reaction pattern

  • Hydrogenation: H₂ with Pt, Pd or Ni gives an alkane; lecturer material describes syn addition.
  • Halogenation, hydrohalogenation and hydration: addition occurs across C=C.
  • Oxidation: cold alkaline KMnO₄ is associated with vicinal diol formation; stronger oxidation can cleave the double bond.
  • Conjugation: alternating double/single bonds have increased stability.
Do not confuse: cis/trans isomerism is not possible for every alkene.
TOPIC 3

Alkynes

Alkyne carbons are sp hybridised and linear, approximately 180°.

Digest it: Lecturer material groups the important reactions as addition of H₂, X₂, HX and H₂O; behaviour as acids; reaction with Ag⁺; and oxidation.

Hydration under acidic mercury-containing conditions first gives an enol, which undergoes keto–enol tautomerism to the carbonyl product.

Terminal alkynes: their terminal hydrogen is relatively acidic, allowing acetylide formation under suitable conditions.

TOPIC 4

Alkyl Halides + Alcohols

SN2: one-step substitution. Nucleophile attack and leaving-group departure occur together; steric hindrance matters.

SN1: stepwise substitution through carbocation formation, so carbocation stability matters.

E2: the base removes a proton from the carbon adjacent to the carbon bearing the leaving group while C–X breaks and C=C forms.

Digest it: Ask: Is there a carbocation intermediate? Is the process one-step? Is substitution or alkene formation occurring?

Alcohols

Know 1°, 2° and 3° classification, hydrogen bonding/solubility trends, dehydration to alkenes and oxidation patterns.

TOPIC 5

Stereochemistry

Focus on chirality, stereogenic centres, enantiomers, diastereomers, meso compounds, R/S configuration, optical activity and racemic mixtures.

Why it matters: lecturer slides connect stereochemistry to biological action: different enantiomers may fit biological receptor sites differently, producing stereospecific activity.
TOPIC 6

Aromatic Compounds

Know the requirements for aromaticity, Hückel's rule and the distinction among aromatic, antiaromatic and non-aromatic systems.

Digest it: Electrophilic aromatic substitution forms a resonance-stabilized σ complex, then loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity.

Be able to recognize electrophiles and the logic of nitration, halogenation and other lecturer-listed substitution reactions.

TOPIC 7

Aldehydes + Ketones

Aldehydes and ketones contain the carbonyl group. Ketones have two alkyl/aryl groups attached to the carbonyl carbon.

Digest it: C=O is polar, making the carbonyl carbon electrophilic. This underlies nucleophilic addition.

Lecturer material includes preparation of aldehydes by oxidation of primary alcohols using PCC, ozonolysis of suitable alkenes and reduction of esters using DIBAH. Reactions with alcohols lead through hemiacetal chemistry.

Know oxidation/reduction, Tollens-type aldehyde recognition and why aldehydes are generally more reactive than ketones.

TOPIC 8

Carboxylic Acids + Derivatives

The functional group is –COOH, the carboxyl group. Naming uses the parent alkane with the –oic acid suffix.

Know hydrogen bonding, boiling-point and solubility trends, weak acidity and formation of carboxylate salts.

Carboxylic acid + Alcohol ⇌ Ester + H₂O
Ester + OH⁻ → Carboxylate salt + Alcohol
Digest it: Under strongly basic ester hydrolysis, the carboxylate salt is formed.
TOPIC 9

Amines

Classify amines as primary, secondary or tertiary according to the number of carbon groups attached to nitrogen. Know common/IUPAC naming and the basic role of the nitrogen lone pair.

Digest it: Basicity is about how available the nitrogen lone pair is to accept H⁺. Aromatic attachment/substitution can change that availability.

Also recognize medicinal amines and the relationship between amines and amide formation.

FINAL CHECK

Comprehensive Final Self-Check

Answer aloud first. Then reveal.

1. Why can suitable alkenes show cis/trans isomerism?
The C=C bond cannot freely rotate because rotation would disrupt the π bond.
2. What geometry is expected around an sp-hybridised carbon?
Linear geometry, about 180°.
3. What is the key mechanistic difference between SN1 and SN2?
SN1 proceeds stepwise through a carbocation; SN2 is a concerted one-step process.
4. What happens during an E2 reaction?
A base removes a β-hydrogen while the leaving group departs and C=C forms in the same step.
5. Give the oxidation sequence for a primary alcohol.
Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid.
6. Why does benzene characteristically undergo substitution rather than ordinary addition?
Substitution allows the ring to regain/preserve aromatic stabilization.
7. What makes the carbonyl carbon susceptible to nucleophilic attack?
The C=O bond is polarized toward oxygen, leaving carbon electron-poor/electrophilic.
8. What products result from base hydrolysis of an ester?
A carboxylate salt and an alcohol.
9. Why are amines basic?
The nitrogen lone pair can accept a proton.
10. Why can stereochemistry change biological activity?
Biological targets are three-dimensional; different stereoisomers can fit receptor sites differently.
