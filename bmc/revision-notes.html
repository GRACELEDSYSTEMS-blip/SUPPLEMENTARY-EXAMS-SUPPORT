⚡ 100 Things You Cannot Enter the BMC Exam Without Knowing

These are not 100 cryptic lines. Each item gives the fact and the meaning you need to answer a question.

Foundations + Alkanes

1Alkanes are saturated.

They contain only C–C and C–H single σ bonds. Open-chain alkanes follow CnH2n+2.

2sp³ means tetrahedral.

An sp³ carbon has four σ bonds arranged at about 109.5°.

3Classify carbon by carbon neighbours.

1° carbon is attached to one carbon, 2° to two, 3° to three and 4° to four.

4Structural isomers change connectivity.

They have the same molecular formula but the atoms are connected differently.

5Longer alkane chain → higher boiling point.

Increasing molecular size strengthens London dispersion forces.

6More branching usually lowers boiling point.

Branched isomers have less surface contact, so intermolecular attractions are weaker.

7Alkanes are hydrophobic.

C–C and C–H bonds are essentially non-polar, so alkanes do not interact strongly with water.

8Complete combustion gives CO₂ + H₂O.

Hydrocarbon + sufficient O₂ produces carbon dioxide and water.

9Halogenation is substitution.

With light/heat, Cl₂ or Br₂ can replace an alkane H with halogen through a free-radical chain reaction.

10Cracking breaks; reforming rearranges.

Cracking produces smaller hydrocarbons. Reforming changes structure and can produce branched, cyclic or aromatic hydrocarbons.

Alkenes

11Alkene carbon is sp².

Each C=C carbon is approximately trigonal planar with bond angles near 120°.

12C=C = one σ + one π bond.

The π bond is more exposed and is the part attacked in many addition reactions.

13C=C cannot freely rotate.

Rotation would disrupt the π overlap; this restricted rotation allows geometrical isomerism.

14Not every alkene has cis/trans isomers.

For the usual cis/trans case, each double-bond carbon must carry two different groups.

15Hydrogenation removes unsaturation.

C=C + H₂ with Pt, Pd or Ni gives an alkane.

16Halogenation adds X₂ across C=C.

The product is a vicinal dihalide with one halogen on each former double-bond carbon.

17Hydrohalogenation adds HX.

H and X add across C=C to produce an alkyl halide.

18Know Markovnikov orientation.

In ordinary ionic HX addition to an unsymmetrical alkene, the pathway favours the more stable carbocation, usually placing X on the more substituted carbon.

19Carbocations can rearrange.

If a more stable carbocation can form by hydride or alkyl shift, the final addition product may have a rearranged skeleton.

20Peroxide can change HBr orientation.

The lecturer notes distinguish ordinary Markovnikov HBr addition from anti-Markovnikov addition in the presence of oxygen/peroxide conditions.

21Hydration gives an alcohol.

Acid-catalysed addition of H and OH across C=C converts an alkene to an alcohol.

22Cold alkaline KMnO₄ gives a vicinal diol.

Two OH groups are added across the double bond.

23Ozonolysis cleaves C=C.

O₃ breaks the double bond and converts the two alkene carbons into carbonyl-containing products.

24Conjugation stabilizes dienes.

Alternating C=C and C–C bonds allow electron delocalization; conjugated systems are more stable than comparable isolated dienes.

25Regioselective ≠ regiospecific.

Regioselective means one constitutional product forms in greater amount; regiospecific means only one orientation is formed.

Alkynes

26Alkyne carbon is sp and linear.

The atoms around C≡C lie approximately 180° apart.

27C≡C contains two π bonds.

A triple bond consists of one σ bond and two π bonds, so it can undergo repeated addition.

28Terminal alkynes are weak acids.

The terminal H is more acidic than alkene/alkane H because the conjugate-base carbon is sp hybridised with high s-character.

29Strong base gives an acetylide ion.

Removing the terminal alkyne H produces RC≡C⁻, a useful carbon nucleophile.

30Full hydrogenation gives an alkane.

Excess H₂ with a metal catalyst reduces C≡C completely.

31Partial reduction can stop at an alkene.

Know the cis/trans distinction: controlled catalytic reduction can give a cis alkene, whereas dissolving-metal conditions can give a trans alkene.

32HX and X₂ add across C≡C.

Because there are two π bonds, addition can occur once or twice depending on reagent amount.

33Alkyne hydration first gives an enol.

Under acid/Hg²⁺ conditions the initial enol rearranges by keto–enol tautomerism to a carbonyl compound.

34Terminal alkynes have a silver-ion reaction.

The lecturer material highlights reaction of terminal alkynes with Ag⁺; recognize this as a test linked to their acidic terminal H.

Alkyl Halides + Alcohols

35Leaving-group ability follows the C–X bond.

C–F is strongest and C–I weakest; iodide generally leaves more readily than bromide, chloride and fluoride.

36SN2 is bimolecular.

Rate depends on both substrate and nucleophile: rate = k[RX][Nu].

37SN2 is one-step.

Nucleophile attack and leaving-group departure occur together through a transition state; no carbocation forms.

38SN2 gives inversion.

Backside attack reverses configuration at a stereogenic reacting carbon.

39Steric hindrance slows SN2.

Methyl and primary substrates are generally more accessible than tertiary substrates.

40SN1 is unimolecular.

The slow step involves substrate ionization, so rate = k[RX].

41SN1 forms a carbocation.

The C–X bond breaks first; the nucleophile attacks the carbocation in a later fast step.

42Carbocation stability favours SN1.

For simple alkyl carbocations, 3° > 2° > 1°; tertiary alkyl halides therefore favour SN1 much more than primary ones.

43E1 and SN1 share the same first step.

Both begin with slow ionization to a carbocation. E1 then loses a proton to make an alkene.

44E2 is one-step.

A base removes β-H as the leaving group departs and the C=C bond forms simultaneously.

45Know α and β carbons in elimination.

The α carbon bears the leaving group; a β carbon adjacent to it supplies the hydrogen removed to form C=C.

46Alcohols hydrogen-bond.

This raises their boiling points compared with similar-mass alkanes.

47Alcohol water solubility falls with chain length.

The OH group is hydrophilic, but the hydrocarbon chain is hydrophobic and eventually dominates.

48Classify alcohol by the OH-bearing carbon.

1°, 2° and 3° alcohols have the OH-bearing carbon attached to one, two or three other carbons respectively.

491° alcohol oxidation has two stages.

1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid if oxidation continues.

50PCC can stop at the aldehyde.

Pyridinium chlorochromate (PCC) oxidizes a primary alcohol under conditions used to isolate the aldehyde instead of continuing to the acid.

512° alcohol → ketone.

Oxidation removes H from the OH-bearing carbon and O–H, forming C=O without changing the carbon skeleton.

52Dehydration of alcohol gives alkene.

H and OH are removed as water, creating a C=C bond; carbocation-forming dehydration can rearrange.

53SOCl₂ converts alcohol to alkyl chloride.

Thionyl chloride replaces OH with Cl and forms SO₂ and HCl as by-products.

54Alcohol + Na releases H₂.

An alcohol can form a sodium alkoxide plus hydrogen gas.

Stereochemistry

55Chiral means non-superimposable mirror image.

A common stereogenic carbon is bonded to four different groups.

56Enantiomers are mirror-image stereoisomers.

They are non-superimposable and have opposite configurations at corresponding stereocentres.

57Diastereomers are not mirror images.

They are stereoisomers with different spatial arrangements but are not an enantiomeric pair.

58Meso compounds are achiral despite stereocentres.

Internal symmetry makes the whole molecule superimposable on its mirror image.

59R/S describes absolute configuration.

Assign priorities by the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog rules, orient the lowest priority away, then trace 1→2→3.

60R does not mean +.

R/S configuration and direction of optical rotation are different properties; one does not predict the other.

61(+) and (−) describe optical rotation.

(+) is clockwise/dextrorotatory; (−) is anticlockwise/laevorotatory.

62Enantiomers share many ordinary physical properties.

In an achiral environment they have the same boiling point, density and refractive index, but rotate plane-polarized light equally in opposite directions.

63Racemic mixture = equal enantiomers.

The opposite rotations cancel, so the mixture is optically inactive overall.

64Stereochemistry matters biologically.

Enzymes and receptors are three-dimensional, so different enantiomers can show very different biological activity.

Aromatic Compounds

65Benzene is unusually stable.

Although highly unsaturated, it does not behave like a normal alkene because its π electrons are delocalized around the ring.

66Aromaticity requires four things.

The system must be cyclic, planar, fully conjugated and contain 4n+2 π electrons.

67Hückel rule = 4n+2 π electrons.

A planar, cyclic, fully conjugated system satisfying this count is aromatic.

68Antiaromatic is not simply “not aromatic.”

A planar cyclic fully conjugated system with 4n π electrons is antiaromatic; a system failing planarity/conjugation is non-aromatic.

69Benzene prefers substitution to addition.

Electrophilic aromatic substitution allows aromaticity to be restored; ordinary addition would destroy it.

70EAS forms a σ complex.

The ring attacks E⁺ to form a resonance-stabilized arenium/σ complex, then loses H⁺ to restore aromaticity.

71Nitration uses NO₂⁺.

The key electrophile is the nitronium ion, generated from the nitrating mixture.

72Halogenation needs Lewis-acid activation.

FeCl₃/FeBr₃-type catalysts help generate the electrophilic halogen species for EAS.

73Friedel–Crafts alkylation adds an alkyl group.

It uses an alkylating electrophile with a Lewis acid such as AlCl₃.

74Friedel–Crafts acylation gives an aryl ketone.

An acid chloride with AlCl₃ generates an acyl electrophile; the product has the aromatic ring attached to a carbonyl-containing acyl group.

75Substituents control orientation and rate.

Some groups activate the ring and direct ortho/para; many electron-withdrawing groups deactivate and direct meta.

76Halogens are the famous exception.

They deactivate the ring but still direct incoming substitution mainly to ortho and para positions.

Aldehydes + Ketones

77Aldehyde = R–CHO; ketone = R–CO–R′.

An aldehyde carbonyl has at least one H; a ketone carbonyl is bonded to two carbon groups.

78Carbonyl carbon is electrophilic.

Oxygen pulls electron density away from C=O, leaving carbon partially positive.

79Nucleophiles attack carbonyl carbon.

Addition converts trigonal-planar C=O carbon into a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate before protonation/workup.

80Aldehydes are generally more reactive than ketones.

They are less sterically crowded and their carbonyl carbon is less electron-rich than in ketones with two alkyl groups.

81Aldehydes oxidize readily.

Oxidation gives carboxylic acid/carboxylate level products; ordinary ketones resist mild oxidation.

82Tollens detects aldehyde behaviour.

Tollens reagent contains Ag⁺ in basic ammonia; aldehyde is oxidized to carboxylate while Ag⁺ becomes metallic silver.

83Reduction: aldehyde → 1° alcohol.

Adding hydrogen equivalents to C=O gives the primary alcohol.

84Reduction: ketone → 2° alcohol.

The carbonyl carbon already has two carbon groups, so the resulting OH-bearing carbon is secondary.

85Hemiacetal has OH and OR on the same carbon.

Further alcohol reaction can replace the OH with another OR to form an acetal.

86DIBAH can reduce an ester to an aldehyde.

Diisobutylaluminium hydride (DIBAH/DIBAL-H), under controlled partial-reduction conditions, stops an ester at the aldehyde level.

87Grignard + formaldehyde → 1° alcohol.

RMgX adds one carbon group to HCHO; acidic workup protonates the alkoxide.

88Grignard + other aldehyde → 2° alcohol.

The carbonyl carbon already has one carbon substituent; RMgX adds the second.

89Grignard + ketone → 3° alcohol.

The ketone carbonyl already has two carbon groups; addition of R supplies the third.

90Grignard + ester uses two equivalents.

Addition occurs twice overall through a ketone intermediate, producing a tertiary alcohol after workup.

91Grignard + CO₂ → carboxylic acid.

After acidic workup, the product has one more carbon than the carbon group originally attached to MgX.

Carboxylic Acids + Derivatives

92Carboxylic acids form resonance-stabilized carboxylates.

Loss of H⁺ gives RCOO⁻, with negative charge shared over two oxygens; this helps explain their acidity.

93Carboxylic acids hydrogen-bond strongly.

They can form dimers, helping explain their high boiling points.

94Water solubility falls as the chain grows.

Short-chain acids are very soluble/miscible, but the hydrophobic hydrocarbon portion becomes more important with length.

95Acid + base gives carboxylate salt.

NaOH deprotonates RCOOH; strong acid can regenerate RCOOH from RCOO⁻.

96NaHCO₃ gives CO₂.

Carboxylic acids react with bicarbonate to form carboxylate salt, water and carbon dioxide gas.

97Esterification: acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water.

Acid-catalysed esterification is reversible; hydrolysis runs the relationship backward.

98Saponification gives carboxylate + alcohol.

Strong-base ester hydrolysis does not leave the acid neutral; it remains as its carboxylate salt until acidified.

99Lactone = cyclic ester; lactam = cyclic amide.

In the ring, O next to C=O identifies the ester pattern; N next to C=O identifies the amide pattern. γ systems commonly form 5-membered rings and δ systems 6-membered rings.

100Know the acyl-derivative family.

Acid chloride has –COCl, ester –COOR, amide –CONR₂-type bonding and anhydride –CO–O–CO–. Acid chlorides/anhydrides are more reactive than esters/amides toward nucleophilic acyl substitution.

Bonus amine essentials — do not skip them

1°, 2°, 3° amines count carbon groups on N.

One, two or three carbon groups directly attached to nitrogen respectively.

Amines are bases and nucleophiles.

The nitrogen lone pair can accept H⁺ and can attack electron-poor centres.

Aryl amines are weaker bases than alkyl amines.

In aniline, the lone pair is delocalized into the aromatic ring and is less available for protonation.

Amides are much less basic than amines.

The nitrogen lone pair is strongly delocalized toward the carbonyl group.

Primary aryl amine + HONO gives a diazonium salt.

NaNO₂/HCl generates nitrous acid; arene diazonium salts are kept cold, about 0–5°C, and are valuable synthetic intermediates.

Secondary amines + HONO form N-nitrosoamines.

The lecturer notes distinguish this from the diazotization behaviour of primary aromatic amines.

Diazonium groups can be replaced.

CuCl, CuBr or CuCN replacement reactions are Sandmeyer reactions; other conditions can replace N₂⁺ with OH, I, F or H.

TOPIC 1

Foundations + Alkanes

Start with structure: alkanes contain only σ bonds and sp³ carbons, so they lack the exposed π electrons that make alkenes and alkynes much more reactive. Their major reactions therefore include combustion and radical substitution rather than ordinary electrophilic addition.

Naming: choose the longest continuous chain, number it to give substituents the lowest possible locants, name substituents and arrange them alphabetically. Structural isomerism begins when the same formula can be connected in more than one way.

Radical halogenation

R–H + X₂ —hν/heat→ R–X + HX

Light or heat initiates radical formation. The important exam idea is that a hydrogen is substituted by halogen; this is not an addition reaction.

TOPIC 2

Alkenes

The C=C π bond is the centre of reactivity. Electrophiles are attracted to the electron-rich π bond, which is why many alkene reactions are electrophilic additions.

Markovnikov HX addition

Unsymmetrical alkene + HX → major alkyl halide

Protonation occurs so that the more stable carbocation is formed. The halide nucleophile then attacks that carbocation. Because carbocations can rearrange, always check whether a more stable carbocation is possible.

Oxidation choices

Cold dilute alkaline KMnO₄ keeps the carbon skeleton and makes a vicinal diol. Ozonolysis breaks the C=C and produces carbonyl fragments. Do not treat these as the same oxidation.

Polymerisation: an alkene π bond can open so many monomers join into a chain. The repeating unit comes from the alkene monomer, while the C=C becomes C–C bonds in the polymer backbone.
TOPIC 3

Alkynes

Because C≡C has two π bonds, alkynes can undergo addition twice. Terminal alkynes are special because their terminal hydrogen can be removed by strong base.

Acetylide formation

RC≡CH + strong base → RC≡C⁻

The acetylide ion is a carbon nucleophile. The greater s-character of sp carbon stabilizes the negative charge better than sp²/sp³ carbon, explaining the higher acidity of terminal alkynes.

Hydration

Alkyne —H₂O, H⁺/Hg²⁺→ enol → carbonyl compound

The initially formed enol is usually not the final isolated product; it tautomerizes to the more stable keto form.

TOPIC 4

Alkyl Halides + Alcohols

Mechanism decision: SN1/E1 involve a carbocation and are helped by carbocation stability. SN2/E2 are concerted one-step processes. SN2 is especially sensitive to steric hindrance; E2 requires a base to remove β-H while the leaving group leaves.

Alcohol oxidation

1° alcohol —PCC→ aldehyde

PCC = pyridinium chlorochromate. It is used when the desired product is the aldehyde rather than further oxidation to a carboxylic acid.

1° alcohol —strong/excess oxidant→ carboxylic acid
2° alcohol —oxidant→ ketone

Making an alkyl chloride from an alcohol

R–OH + SOCl₂ → R–Cl + SO₂ + HCl

SOCl₂ = thionyl chloride. It replaces the alcohol OH group with chlorine.

TOPIC 5

Stereochemistry

Do not equate R/S with +/−. R/S describes spatial configuration from priority rules; +/− is an experimental observation of how the compound rotates plane-polarized light.

Meso trap: having stereocentres does not automatically make a molecule chiral. Internal symmetry can make the entire molecule achiral.

Biological systems are chiral. The lecturer examples emphasize that enantiomers can differ markedly in metabolism or pharmacological activity even when their ordinary physical properties are almost identical.

TOPIC 6

Aromatic Compounds

Aromaticity: cyclic + planar + fully conjugated + 4n+2 π electrons. Benzene has six π electrons and is the standard aromatic example.

Electrophilic aromatic substitution

Step 1: generate a strong electrophile. Step 2: aromatic π electrons attack E⁺ to form the resonance-stabilized σ complex/arenium ion; this is usually the slow step. Step 3: loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity.

Directing effects

Existing substituents change both rate and position of further EAS. Electron-donating groups generally activate and direct ortho/para. Strong electron-withdrawing groups generally deactivate and direct meta. Halogens are deactivating but ortho/para directing.

TOPIC 7

Aldehydes + Ketones

The polar C=O bond explains both physical properties and reactivity: oxygen is δ− and carbon is δ+, so nucleophiles attack carbon.

Preparing aldehydes

Primary alcohol + PCC → aldehyde. Ozonolysis of a suitable alkene can produce aldehydes depending on substitution/workup. Ester + DIBAH/DIBAL-H → aldehyde under controlled partial reduction. DIBAH means diisobutylaluminium hydride.

Tollens test

Aldehyde + Tollens reagent → carboxylate + Ag(s)

Tollens reagent is a mild Ag⁺ oxidizing system in basic ammonia. The aldehyde is oxidized while silver ion is reduced, producing the characteristic silver deposit.

Hemiacetal → acetal

Alcohol addition can give a hemiacetal, where the same carbon carries OH and OR. Further reaction with alcohol under suitable acid conditions gives an acetal, where that carbon carries two OR groups.

Grignard reactions

RMgX behaves as a strong carbon nucleophile. R attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon; acidic aqueous workup protonates the alkoxide.

HCHO + RMgX → 1° alcohol
R′CHO + RMgX → 2° alcohol
R′COR″ + RMgX → 3° alcohol
Ester + 2 RMgX → 3° alcohol
CO₂ + RMgX → carboxylic acid after H₃O⁺
Why the pattern works: count how many carbon groups the carbonyl carbon already has. Formaldehyde has none, another aldehyde has one and a ketone has two; Grignard adds another carbon group.
TOPIC 8

Carboxylic Acids + Derivatives

Carboxylic acids are more acidic than alcohols because their conjugate base, the carboxylate ion, is resonance stabilized over two oxygens.

Acid-base behaviour

RCOOH + OH⁻ → RCOO⁻ + H₂O

Strong base forms a carboxylate salt. Strong acid reverses this and regenerates RCOOH. Bicarbonate also deprotonates carboxylic acids and releases CO₂.

Esterification vs hydrolysis

RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ + H₂O

Acid-catalysed esterification forms the ester. Acid hydrolysis returns carboxylic acid + alcohol. Base hydrolysis gives carboxylate salt + alcohol and is called saponification.

Acid derivatives

The group attached to the acyl carbon identifies the derivative: –Cl acid chloride; –OR ester; –NR₂ amide; –OCO– anhydride. Acid chlorides are activated because chloride is a good leaving group. The lecturer notes place acid chlorides and anhydrides above esters and amides in acyl-substitution reactivity.

Lactones and lactams

Lactone = cyclic ester; its ring contains C(=O)–O. Lactam = cyclic amide; its ring contains C(=O)–N. γ systems commonly form 5-membered rings and δ systems 6-membered rings. Hydrolysis opens the ring toward hydroxy-acid-type products for lactones and amino-acid-type products for lactams.

TOPIC 9

Amines

Amines are bases and nucleophiles because nitrogen has a lone pair. Primary, secondary and tertiary classification counts carbon groups attached directly to N.

Basicity: aryl amines such as aniline are weaker bases than alkyl amines because the lone pair is delocalized into the aromatic ring. Amides are even less basic because the N lone pair is strongly delocalized toward C=O.

Nitrous acid reactions

Nitrous acid is generated in situ from NaNO₂ + HCl. Primary aromatic amines form arenediazonium salts at about 0–5°C. Secondary amines form N-nitrosoamines. Diazonium salts are useful because N₂⁺ can be replaced by other groups.

Sandmeyer reactions

Replacement of the diazonium group by Cl, Br or CN using CuCl, CuBr or CuCN is classically called a Sandmeyer reaction. Nitrogen gas leaves, which strongly drives the transformation.

CONNECT IT

Reaction Pathways — With the Meaning Included

Alkane → Alkyl halide → Alcohol → Alkene

Halogenation replaces alkane H with X. Nucleophilic substitution then lets OH⁻ replace X to give an alcohol. Dehydration removes H₂O from the alcohol to form C=C. The reverse alkene-to-alcohol step is hydration.

1° Alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid

Oxidation first forms C=O while the carbon still carries H, giving an aldehyde. Continued oxidation converts the aldehyde to the carboxylic-acid level. PCC is useful when you want to isolate the aldehyde.

2° Alcohol → Ketone

The OH-bearing carbon has two carbon groups. Oxidation forms C=O, so the product is a ketone rather than an aldehyde.

Aldehyde/Ketone → Alcohol

Reduction adds hydrogen equivalents to C=O. Aldehyde gives 1° alcohol; ketone gives 2° alcohol. NaBH₄ and LiAlH₄ are important reagents to recognize.

Carboxylic acid + Alcohol ⇌ Ester

Esterification forms ester + water. Acid hydrolysis reverses it. Strong-base hydrolysis instead traps the acid part as a carboxylate salt: this is saponification.

Carbonyl + Grignard → new C–C bond

RMgX supplies a carbon nucleophile, so Grignard chemistry is important because it builds the carbon skeleton, not merely because it converts one oxygen functional group into another.

FINISH HERE

Comprehensive Final Self-Check

Answer aloud before revealing. If an answer feels unfamiliar, use ☰ Topics to jump back immediately.

1. Why are alkenes generally more reactive than alkanes?
Alkenes contain an exposed π bond. Its electrons are more accessible to electrophiles than the σ bonds of alkanes.
2. What is the key difference between SN1 and SN2?
SN1 is stepwise through a carbocation and its rate depends mainly on substrate. SN2 is concerted, depends on both substrate and nucleophile, and gives backside-attack inversion.
3. What is the key difference between E1 and E2?
E1 forms a carbocation first, then loses H⁺. E2 removes β-H while the leaving group leaves and C=C forms in one step.
4. Explain the complete oxidation pathway of a primary alcohol.
1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid. PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate) is used when the aim is to stop at the aldehyde stage.
5. What does a secondary alcohol give on oxidation?
A ketone.
6. Why does benzene prefer substitution to addition?
Substitution allows aromaticity to be restored, whereas ordinary addition would destroy the aromatic π system.
7. State the four requirements for aromaticity.
Cyclic, planar, fully conjugated and 4n+2 π electrons.
8. Why are aldehydes generally more reactive than ketones toward nucleophilic addition?
Aldehydes are less sterically crowded and usually have a more electrophilic carbonyl carbon because they have fewer electron-donating alkyl groups.
9. What happens in a positive Tollens test?
The aldehyde is oxidized to carboxylate level while Ag⁺ is reduced to metallic silver, producing the silver deposit/mirror.
10. Define a hemiacetal and an acetal.
A hemiacetal has OH and OR on the same carbon. An acetal has two OR groups on that carbon.
11. Give the Grignard product pattern for formaldehyde, aldehyde and ketone.
After acidic workup: formaldehyde → 1° alcohol; other aldehyde → 2° alcohol; ketone → 3° alcohol.
12. Why does an ester require two equivalents of Grignard reagent?
The first addition ultimately gives a ketone intermediate, and that ketone undergoes a second Grignard addition. Workup gives a tertiary alcohol.
13. What does RMgX + CO₂ give after acidic workup?
A carboxylic acid containing one more carbon than the carbon group originally attached to MgX.
14. What is DIBAH/DIBAL-H?
Diisobutylaluminium hydride. Under controlled conditions it can partially reduce an ester to an aldehyde.
15. Distinguish lactone from lactam.
A lactone is a cyclic ester containing C(=O)–O in the ring; a lactam is a cyclic amide containing C(=O)–N in the ring.
16. What are the products of acid versus base hydrolysis of an ester?
Acid hydrolysis gives carboxylic acid + alcohol. Base hydrolysis gives carboxylate salt + alcohol and is called saponification.
17. Why are carboxylic acids more acidic than alcohols?
Their conjugate base, the carboxylate ion, is resonance stabilized over two oxygen atoms.
18. Why is aniline less basic than an alkyl amine?
The nitrogen lone pair is delocalized into the aromatic ring and is therefore less available to accept H⁺.
19. What happens when a primary aromatic amine reacts with nitrous acid at 0–5°C?
It forms an arenediazonium salt.
20. What is a Sandmeyer reaction?
Replacement of an aromatic diazonium group by Cl, Br or CN using copper(I) salts such as CuCl, CuBr or CuCN, with loss of N₂.
