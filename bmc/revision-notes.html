⚡ BMC Last-Minute Essentials

A focused final review of the concepts, reactions and distinctions worth checking before the exam.

Foundations + Alkanes

1Alkanes are saturated.

They contain only C–C and C–H single σ bonds. Open-chain alkanes follow CnH2n+2.

2sp³ means tetrahedral.

An sp³ carbon has four σ bonds arranged at about 109.5°.

3Classify carbon by carbon neighbours.

1° carbon is attached to one carbon, 2° to two, 3° to three and 4° to four.

4Structural isomers change connectivity.

They have the same molecular formula but the atoms are connected differently.

5Longer alkane chain → higher boiling point.

Increasing molecular size strengthens London dispersion forces.

6More branching usually lowers boiling point.

Branched isomers have less surface contact, so intermolecular attractions are weaker.

7Alkanes are hydrophobic.

C–C and C–H bonds are essentially non-polar, so alkanes do not interact strongly with water.

8Complete combustion gives CO₂ + H₂O.

Hydrocarbon + sufficient O₂ produces carbon dioxide and water.

9Halogenation is substitution.

With light/heat, Cl₂ or Br₂ can replace an alkane H with halogen through a free-radical chain reaction.

10Cracking breaks; reforming rearranges.

Cracking produces smaller hydrocarbons. Reforming changes structure and can produce branched, cyclic or aromatic hydrocarbons.

Alkenes

11Alkene carbon is sp².

Each C=C carbon is approximately trigonal planar with bond angles near 120°.

12C=C = one σ + one π bond.

The π bond is more exposed and is the part attacked in many addition reactions.

13C=C cannot freely rotate.

Rotation would disrupt the π overlap; this restricted rotation allows geometrical isomerism.

14Not every alkene has cis/trans isomers.

For the usual cis/trans case, each double-bond carbon must carry two different groups.

15Hydrogenation removes unsaturation.

C=C + H₂ with Pt, Pd or Ni gives an alkane.

16Halogenation adds X₂ across C=C.

The product is a vicinal dihalide with one halogen on each former double-bond carbon.

17Hydrohalogenation adds HX.

H and X add across C=C to produce an alkyl halide.

18Know Markovnikov orientation.

In ordinary ionic HX addition to an unsymmetrical alkene, the pathway favours the more stable carbocation, usually placing X on the more substituted carbon.

19Carbocations can rearrange.

If a more stable carbocation can form by hydride or alkyl shift, the final addition product may have a rearranged skeleton.

20Peroxide can change HBr orientation.

The lecturer notes distinguish ordinary Markovnikov HBr addition from anti-Markovnikov addition in the presence of oxygen/peroxide conditions.

21Hydration gives an alcohol.

Acid-catalysed addition of H and OH across C=C converts an alkene to an alcohol.

22Cold alkaline KMnO₄ gives a vicinal diol.

Two OH groups are added across the double bond.

23Ozonolysis cleaves C=C.

O₃ breaks the double bond and converts the two alkene carbons into carbonyl-containing products.

24Conjugation stabilizes dienes.

Alternating C=C and C–C bonds allow electron delocalization; conjugated systems are more stable than comparable isolated dienes.

25Regioselective ≠ regiospecific.

Regioselective means one constitutional product forms in greater amount; regiospecific means only one orientation is formed.

Alkynes

26Alkyne carbon is sp and linear.

The atoms around C≡C lie approximately 180° apart.

27C≡C contains two π bonds.

A triple bond consists of one σ bond and two π bonds, so it can undergo repeated addition.

28Terminal alkynes are weak acids.

The terminal H is more acidic than alkene/alkane H because the conjugate-base carbon is sp hybridised with high s-character.

29Strong base gives an acetylide ion.

Removing the terminal alkyne H produces RC≡C⁻, a useful carbon nucleophile.

30Full hydrogenation gives an alkane.

Excess H₂ with a metal catalyst reduces C≡C completely.

31Partial reduction can stop at an alkene.

Know the cis/trans distinction: controlled catalytic reduction can give a cis alkene, whereas dissolving-metal conditions can give a trans alkene.

32HX and X₂ add across C≡C.

Because there are two π bonds, addition can occur once or twice depending on reagent amount.

33Alkyne hydration first gives an enol.

Under acid/Hg²⁺ conditions the initial enol rearranges by keto–enol tautomerism to a carbonyl compound.

34Terminal alkynes have a silver-ion reaction.

The lecturer material highlights reaction of terminal alkynes with Ag⁺; recognize this as a test linked to their acidic terminal H.

Alkyl Halides + Alcohols

35Leaving-group ability follows the C–X bond.

C–F is strongest and C–I weakest; iodide generally leaves more readily than bromide, chloride and fluoride.

36SN2 is bimolecular.

Rate depends on both substrate and nucleophile: rate = k[RX][Nu].

37SN2 is one-step.

Nucleophile attack and leaving-group departure occur together through a transition state.

38SN2 gives inversion.

Backside attack reverses the configuration at a stereogenic reacting carbon.

39Steric hindrance slows SN2.

Methyl/primary substrates are generally more accessible than tertiary substrates.

40SN1 is unimolecular.

Its rate-determining ionization depends on substrate concentration: rate = k[RX].

41SN1 forms a carbocation.

The leaving group departs first; nucleophile attack follows.

42SN1 favours stable carbocations.

Tertiary substrates are usually better than primary substrates for SN1 because tertiary carbocations are more stable.

43SN1 can rearrange.

Because a real carbocation intermediate exists, hydride/alkyl shifts may occur before nucleophilic attack.

44E2 is concerted.

Base removes β-H while the leaving group departs and C=C forms in the same step.

45E1 forms a carbocation first.

Leaving-group departure precedes deprotonation and alkene formation.

46Substitution and elimination compete.

Nucleophile/base strength, substrate structure, solvent and temperature influence which pathway dominates.

47Classify alcohol at the OH-bearing carbon.

1°, 2° and 3° describe how many carbon groups are attached to the carbon bearing –OH.

48Alcohols hydrogen-bond.

The O–H group raises boiling point relative to similar hydrocarbons and helps small alcohols dissolve in water.

49Alcohol dehydration gives an alkene.

Loss of H₂O creates C=C.

501° and 2° alcohol oxidation differs.

1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; 2° alcohol → ketone.

Stereochemistry

51A stereogenic carbon usually has four different groups.

Such a tetrahedral carbon can give non-superimposable mirror-image arrangements.

52Enantiomers are mirror images.

They are non-superimposable mirror-image stereoisomers.

53Diastereomers are not mirror images.

They are stereoisomers with a non-mirror-image relationship.

54Meso compounds are achiral despite stereocentres.

Internal symmetry makes the molecule superimposable on its mirror image.

55R/S describes absolute configuration.

Use CIP priorities; with priority 4 pointing away, clockwise 1→2→3 is R and anticlockwise is S.

56R/S does not tell +/− rotation.

Absolute configuration and direction of optical rotation are different pieces of information.

57(+) is dextrorotatory; (−) is levorotatory.

(+) rotates plane-polarized light clockwise and (−) anticlockwise.

58A racemate is optically inactive overall.

Equal amounts of two enantiomers give equal and opposite rotations that cancel.

59Enantiomers share most achiral physical properties.

They can have the same boiling point, density and refractive index but opposite optical rotation.

60Stereochemistry can change biological activity.

Biological targets are three-dimensional, so different stereoisomers can interact differently with receptors and enzymes.

Aromatic Compounds

61Aromatic systems are cyclic, planar and conjugated.

A continuous ring of p orbitals is needed for delocalization.

62Hückel rule = 4n+2 π electrons.

A planar fully conjugated ring satisfying 4n+2 π electrons is aromatic.

634n π electrons can be antiaromatic.

If the ring is cyclic, planar and fully conjugated but has 4n π electrons, it is antiaromatic.

64Non-aromatic means a requirement is missing.

Loss of planarity, cyclic conjugation or continuous p orbitals prevents aromaticity.

65Benzene prefers substitution.

Electrophilic aromatic substitution allows aromaticity to be restored; ordinary addition would sacrifice aromatic stabilization.

66EAS forms a σ complex.

The ring attacks an electrophile, temporarily loses aromaticity, then loses H⁺ to restore the aromatic system.

67Nitration uses a nitronium electrophile.

Concentrated HNO₃/H₂SO₄ generates NO₂⁺, which attacks benzene.

68Halogenation needs a Lewis-acid catalyst.

Br₂/FeBr₃ or Cl₂/FeCl₃ generates a stronger electrophilic halogenating species.

69Friedel–Crafts alkylation adds an alkyl group.

An alkyl halide with AlCl₃ generates the electrophilic species used to alkylate the aromatic ring.

70Friedel–Crafts acylation adds an acyl group.

An acyl halide/AlCl₃ produces an acylium-type electrophile and gives an aromatic ketone.

71Activating groups usually direct ortho/para.

Electron-donating substituents generally make the ring more reactive and favour ortho/para substitution.

72Strong electron-withdrawing groups usually direct meta.

They deactivate the ring and destabilize ortho/para σ-complex pathways.

Aldehydes + Ketones

73Carbonyl carbon is sp² and electrophilic.

The polarized C=O bond leaves carbon partially positive and vulnerable to nucleophilic attack.

74Aldehyde has at least one H on carbonyl carbon.

General form R–CHO; a ketone has two carbon groups on the carbonyl carbon.

75PCC means pyridinium chlorochromate.

It can oxidize a primary alcohol to an aldehyde while avoiding the usual further oxidation to a carboxylic acid.

762° alcohol oxidation gives a ketone.

The OH-bearing carbon becomes C=O without changing the carbon skeleton.

77DIBAH/DIBAL-H means diisobutylaluminium hydride.

Under controlled conditions it can partially reduce an ester to an aldehyde.

78Aldehydes oxidize more readily than ketones.

An aldehyde can be oxidized to a carboxylic acid; ordinary ketones resist the same mild oxidation.

79Tollens recognizes aldehydes.

The aldehyde is oxidized while Ag⁺ is reduced to metallic silver, producing the familiar silver-mirror response.

80Aldehyde reduction gives a 1° alcohol.

NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄ can reduce C=O to C–OH.

81Ketone reduction gives a 2° alcohol.

The carbonyl carbon retains its two carbon substituents and gains H/OH.

82Aldehydes are usually more reactive than ketones.

They are less sterically crowded and generally have a more electrophilic carbonyl carbon.

83Hemiacetal = OH and OR on the same carbon.

It forms after one alcohol adds to a carbonyl under suitable conditions.

84Acetal has two OR groups on the same carbon.

Further alcohol reaction under acid conditions replaces the hemiacetal OH by OR.

85Grignard reagent is RMgX.

Its carbon behaves as a strong nucleophile and attacks electrophilic carbonyl carbon.

86Formaldehyde + RMgX → 1° alcohol.

After acidic workup, the Grignard carbon has been added to formaldehyde.

87Other aldehyde + RMgX → 2° alcohol.

The former carbonyl carbon ends up attached to OH, the original carbon group and the incoming Grignard carbon group.

88Ketone + RMgX → 3° alcohol.

The former carbonyl carbon already has two carbon groups and receives a third from RMgX.

89Ester + 2 RMgX → 3° alcohol.

The first addition gives a ketone intermediate; a second Grignard addition occurs before workup.

90RMgX + CO₂ → carboxylic acid.

Acidic workup gives RCOOH and extends the Grignard carbon skeleton by one carbon.

Carboxylic Acids + Derivatives

91Carboxyl group = –COOH.

It combines a carbonyl and hydroxyl group on the same carbon.

92Carboxylate is resonance stabilized.

After H⁺ loss, the negative charge is delocalized over two oxygens; this helps explain carboxylic-acid acidity.

93Carboxylic acids hydrogen-bond strongly.

They can associate as dimers, helping explain their relatively high boiling points.

94Water solubility falls as the carbon chain grows.

The polar –COOH becomes a smaller part of the molecule as the hydrophobic hydrocarbon portion increases.

95Acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water.

This is esterification; acid hydrolysis reverses it.

96Basic ester hydrolysis = saponification.

It gives a carboxylate salt plus an alcohol.

97Recognize the acid derivatives.

Acid chloride = –COCl; ester = –COOR; amide = –CONH₂/–CONR₂; anhydride contains two acyl groups linked through O.

98Acid chlorides are highly reactive derivatives.

Chloride is a good leaving group, so acid chlorides undergo nucleophilic acyl substitution readily.

99Lactone = cyclic ester; lactam = cyclic amide.

Look for O or N adjacent to the carbonyl as part of the ring. Hydrolysis opens the ring to the corresponding hydroxy-acid or amino-acid type product.

100γ and δ describe ring-forming position.

γ-lactones/lactams commonly have five-membered rings; δ forms commonly have six-membered rings.

Amines — do not skip these

Classify by groups attached to nitrogen.

1° amine has one carbon group on N, 2° has two and 3° has three.

Amines are bases and nucleophiles.

The nitrogen lone pair can accept H⁺ or attack an electrophilic carbon.

Aromatic amines are usually less basic than aliphatic amines.

In aniline-type compounds the lone pair can delocalize into the aromatic ring, making it less available to bind H⁺.

Acylation gives amides.

An amine can react with an acid chloride or related acyl derivative to form an amide.

Primary aromatic amines can form diazonium salts.

With nitrous acid at low temperature, aniline-type amines form aromatic diazonium salts.

Diazonium salts are synthetic intermediates.

They can undergo replacement reactions; Sandmeyer-type chemistry replaces N₂⁺ using Cu(I) salts such as CuCl or CuBr.

High-Yield Reaction Pathways — Understand These

Alkane → alkyl halide

Alkane + Cl₂/Br₂ —light/heat→ Alkyl halide + HX

Free-radical substitution replaces an alkane H with halogen.

Alkyl halide → alcohol

R–X + OH⁻ → R–OH + X⁻

Hydroxide acts as a nucleophile and replaces the leaving group.

Alcohol ⇄ alkene

Alcohol —dehydration→ Alkene + H₂O

Dehydration removes H and OH as water. Alkene hydration performs the reverse functional-group change by adding H and OH across C=C.

Alcohol oxidation

1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid

The first oxidation product is an aldehyde; continued oxidation gives a carboxylic acid. PCC is used when the aldehyde needs to be isolated.

2° alcohol → ketone

A secondary alcohol gives a ketone, not an aldehyde.

Esterification / hydrolysis

Carboxylic acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + H₂O

Acid-catalysed esterification joins the two reactants. Acid hydrolysis reverses it; basic hydrolysis gives carboxylate salt + alcohol and is called saponification.

TOPIC 1

Foundations + Alkanes

Alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons. Their relatively non-polar C–C and C–H bonds explain their hydrophobic character and relatively low reactivity toward many polar reagents.

Naming: choose the longest parent chain, number for the lowest substituent positions, name substituents and alphabetize them. Repeated substituents use di-, tri-, tetra-.

Physical trends: increasing molecular size generally raises boiling point; branching among isomers generally lowers it. Complete combustion gives CO₂ and H₂O. Halogenation is a free-radical substitution process.

TOPIC 2

Alkenes

C=C has one σ and one π bond. The π bond is the reactive part and prevents free rotation.

Hydrogenation

C=C + H₂ —Pt/Pd/Ni→ Alkane

The π bond is removed as H adds to both alkene carbons.

HX addition

Alkene + HX → Alkyl halide

For unsymmetrical alkenes, ordinary ionic addition follows the more stable carbocation pathway and gives the Markovnikov major product. Carbocation rearrangement may change the skeleton.

Oxidation

Cold alkaline potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) gives a vicinal diol. Ozonolysis (O₃) cleaves C=C into carbonyl products.

TOPIC 3

Alkynes

C≡C carbons are sp/linear. Terminal alkynes are weak acids and can form acetylide ions with sufficiently strong bases.

Hydration

Acid/Hg²⁺-assisted hydration initially forms an enol. Keto–enol tautomerism then converts it to the more stable carbonyl compound.

Reduction

Complete hydrogenation gives an alkane. Controlled partial reduction can stop at an alkene; know the cis-versus-trans product distinction from the lecturer material.

TOPIC 4

Alkyl Halides + Alcohols

SN2

One-step backside nucleophilic attack. Rate depends on substrate and nucleophile. Steric crowding slows the reaction and a stereogenic reacting carbon undergoes inversion.

SN1

Stepwise. The leaving group first forms a carbocation; the nucleophile then attacks. Carbocation stability and rearrangement matter.

E2

One-step elimination: base removes β-H while leaving group departs and C=C forms.

E1

Stepwise elimination through a carbocation, followed by proton loss to make the alkene.

Alcohol oxidation: primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid; secondary alcohol → ketone. Dehydration of an alcohol produces an alkene.
TOPIC 5

Stereochemistry

Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images. Diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images. Meso compounds can contain stereocentres but remain achiral because of internal symmetry.

R/S: rank groups using CIP priorities. Put priority 4 away; clockwise 1→2→3 is R and anticlockwise is S. Do not infer optical rotation from R/S.

Biological receptors and enzymes are three-dimensional, so different stereoisomers can have very different biological effects.

TOPIC 6

Aromatic Compounds

Aromaticity requires a cyclic, planar, continuously conjugated system satisfying 4n+2 π electrons.

Electrophilic aromatic substitution

The aromatic ring attacks an electrophile to form a resonance-stabilized σ complex. Loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity.

Nitration: HNO₃/H₂SO₄ generates NO₂⁺. Halogenation: X₂/FeX₃ activates halogen. Friedel–Crafts alkylation: alkyl halide/AlCl₃. Friedel–Crafts acylation: acyl halide/AlCl₃.

Directing effects: electron-donating/activating groups usually direct ortho/para; strong electron-withdrawing/deactivating groups usually direct meta.
TOPIC 7

Aldehydes + Ketones

The C=O bond is polarized, so the carbonyl carbon is electrophilic and susceptible to nucleophilic attack.

Preparation and redox

PCC oxidation

1° Alcohol —PCC→ Aldehyde

PCC = pyridinium chlorochromate. It is useful when oxidation should stop at the aldehyde rather than continue to a carboxylic acid.

DIBAH partial reduction

Ester —DIBAH/DIBAL-H→ Aldehyde

DIBAH/DIBAL-H = diisobutylaluminium hydride. Under controlled conditions it partially reduces an ester to an aldehyde.

Tollens' reagent oxidizes an aldehyde while Ag⁺ is reduced to metallic silver. NaBH₄/LiAlH₄ reduce aldehydes to 1° alcohols and ketones to 2° alcohols.

Alcohol addition

A hemiacetal has OH and OR on the same carbon. Further alcohol reaction under suitable acid conditions gives an acetal, which has two OR groups on that carbon.

Grignard product pattern

RMgX supplies a carbon nucleophile. After carbonyl attack and acidic workup:

  • Formaldehyde → 1° alcohol.
  • Other aldehyde → 2° alcohol.
  • Ketone → 3° alcohol.
  • Ester + two equivalents RMgX → 3° alcohol.
  • CO₂ → carboxylic acid, adding one carbon to the Grignard carbon skeleton.
TOPIC 8

Carboxylic Acids + Derivatives

Carboxylic acids form resonance-stabilized carboxylate ions after losing H⁺. Their strong hydrogen bonding gives relatively high boiling points.

Esterification

RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ + H₂O

A carboxylic acid and alcohol form an ester. Acid hydrolysis reverses this.

Saponification

Ester + OH⁻ → Carboxylate salt + Alcohol

This is basic ester hydrolysis.

Acid derivatives: acid chloride (–COCl), ester (–COOR), amide (–CONH₂/related N-substitution) and acid anhydride. Acid chlorides are particularly reactive because chloride is a good leaving group.

Lactone = cyclic ester. Lactam = cyclic amide. γ forms are commonly five-membered rings and δ forms six-membered rings. Hydrolysis opens the ring.
TOPIC 9

Amines

Classify amines by the number of carbon groups directly attached to nitrogen: primary, secondary or tertiary.

The nitrogen lone pair explains both basicity and nucleophilicity. Aromatic amines such as aniline are generally less basic than comparable aliphatic amines because the lone pair can delocalize into the aromatic ring.

Amide formation

An amine can attack an activated carboxylic-acid derivative such as an acid chloride to form an amide.

Diazonium chemistry

A primary aromatic amine can react with nitrous acid at low temperature to form a diazonium salt. Diazonium groups can then be replaced in reactions such as Sandmeyer chemistry.

CONNECT IT

Reaction Pathways — With the Meaning Included

Primary alcohol oxidation

1° Alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid

Oxidation first removes hydrogen/raises oxidation at the OH-bearing carbon to make C=O. An aldehyde still has a hydrogen on that carbon and can therefore be oxidized further to the carboxylic-acid level. PCC is used when the reaction should stop at the aldehyde.

Secondary alcohol oxidation

2° Alcohol → Ketone

The OH-bearing carbon becomes C=O. Because that carbon already has two carbon substituents, the product is a ketone.

Carbonyl reduction

Aldehyde → 1° Alcohol   |   Ketone → 2° Alcohol

Reduction changes C=O back toward C–OH. The number of carbon groups already attached to the carbonyl carbon determines the alcohol class.

Alkene → alcohol

C=C + H₂O/H⁺ → Alcohol

Hydration adds H and OH across the double bond. This is why alkene and alcohol chemistry are directly connected.

Acid derivative → amide

Activated acyl compound + Amine → Amide

The amine nitrogen attacks the acyl carbon and a leaving group is replaced. This is nucleophilic acyl substitution.

Comprehensive Final Self-Check

Answer aloud first. Then reveal the answer. Each reveal also gives the nearby fact or distinction most worth remembering.

1. What hybridisation and geometry occur at an alkene carbon?
Answer: sp² and trigonal planar, with bond angles around 120°.
Also remember: C=C contains one σ and one π bond. The π bond prevents free rotation and is responsible for much of alkene addition chemistry.
2. Why can suitable alkenes show cis/trans isomerism?
Answer: The π bond prevents free rotation around C=C, locking substituents into different spatial arrangements.
Also remember: not every alkene qualifies. In the usual cis/trans case, each double-bond carbon must carry two different groups.
3. What does cold alkaline KMnO₄ do to an alkene?
Answer: It adds two OH groups across C=C to form a vicinal diol.
Also remember: do not confuse this with ozonolysis, which cleaves the C=C bond to carbonyl-containing products.
4. Why is the terminal hydrogen of an alkyne more acidic than an alkene or alkane hydrogen?
Answer: The conjugate-base carbon is sp hybridised and has greater s-character, which stabilizes the negative charge better.
Also remember: sufficiently strong base forms an acetylide ion, RC≡C⁻, which can act as a carbon nucleophile.
5. Distinguish SN1 from SN2.
Answer: SN1 is stepwise and forms a carbocation; SN2 is concerted and occurs in one step.
Also remember: SN2 gives backside attack/inversion and is slowed by steric hindrance. SN1 can rearrange and is favoured by substrates capable of forming stable carbocations.
6. What happens in an E2 reaction?
Answer: A base removes a β-hydrogen while the leaving group departs and C=C forms in the same step.
Also remember: E2 is concerted; E1 instead forms a carbocation first and then loses a proton to form the alkene.
7. What are the oxidation products of primary and secondary alcohols?
Answer: A primary alcohol gives an aldehyde and can be oxidized further to a carboxylic acid. A secondary alcohol gives a ketone.
Also remember: PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate) is useful when a primary alcohol should stop at the aldehyde stage.
8. What do aldehydes and ketones give on reduction?
Answer: Aldehyde → primary alcohol; ketone → secondary alcohol.
Also remember: NaBH₄ and LiAlH₄ are important carbonyl-reducing agents. The alcohol class follows from how many carbon groups were already attached to the carbonyl carbon.
9. What does Tollens' reagent detect?
Answer: It is used to recognize aldehydes; the aldehyde is oxidized while Ag⁺ is reduced to metallic silver.
Also remember: ordinary ketones do not show the same aldehyde response under this mild test.
10. Why are aldehydes generally more reactive than ketones toward nucleophilic addition?
Answer: Aldehydes are less sterically crowded and their carbonyl carbon is generally more electrophilic.
Also remember: ketones have two carbon groups around the carbonyl carbon; these increase crowding and donate electron density compared with an aldehyde.
11. What is a hemiacetal, and how does it differ from an acetal?
Answer: A hemiacetal has OH and OR on the same carbon; an acetal has two OR groups on that carbon.
Also remember: a hemiacetal is the intermediate after one alcohol addition to a carbonyl; further alcohol reaction under suitable acid conditions produces the acetal.
12. State the Grignard product pattern for formaldehyde, an aldehyde and a ketone.
Answer: After acidic workup: formaldehyde → 1° alcohol; other aldehyde → 2° alcohol; ketone → 3° alcohol.
Also remember: RMgX behaves as a carbon nucleophile. The incoming R group becomes a new C–C bond at the former carbonyl carbon.
13. What happens when an ester reacts with two equivalents of a Grignard reagent?
Answer: A tertiary alcohol forms after acidic workup.
Also remember: addition occurs twice overall. The first addition produces a ketone intermediate, which is attacked by a second equivalent of RMgX.
14. What does RMgX + CO₂ produce after acidic workup?
Answer: A carboxylic acid, RCOOH.
Also remember: this reaction adds one carbon to the carbon skeleton carried by the Grignard reagent.
15. What is the difference between a lactone and a lactam?
Answer: A lactone is a cyclic ester; a lactam is a cyclic amide.
Also remember: look for O versus N next to the carbonyl within the ring. γ forms are commonly five-membered rings and δ forms six-membered rings; hydrolysis opens the ring.
16. State Hückel's rule for aromaticity.
Answer: A cyclic, planar, fully conjugated system is aromatic when it contains 4n+2 π electrons.
Also remember: a cyclic, planar, fully conjugated 4n π-electron system is antiaromatic; if one of those structural requirements is missing, the system is non-aromatic.
17. Why does benzene usually undergo substitution rather than ordinary addition?
Answer: Substitution allows aromaticity to be restored, whereas ordinary addition would destroy the aromatic π system.
Also remember: electrophilic aromatic substitution proceeds through a σ complex, followed by loss of H⁺ to restore aromaticity.
18. What is saponification?
Answer: Basic hydrolysis of an ester to give a carboxylate salt and an alcohol.
Also remember: acid hydrolysis instead gives the neutral carboxylic acid plus alcohol. A sodium or potassium salt of a long-chain fatty acid is a soap.
19. Why are carboxylic acids acidic?
Answer: Loss of H⁺ gives a carboxylate ion whose negative charge is resonance stabilized over two oxygen atoms.
Also remember: carboxylic acids also hydrogen-bond strongly, helping explain their relatively high boiling points; water solubility decreases as the hydrocarbon chain becomes longer.
20. Why are amines basic?
Answer: The nitrogen lone pair can accept H⁺.
Also remember: the same lone pair makes amines nucleophilic. Aromatic amines are generally less basic than comparable aliphatic amines because the lone pair can delocalize into the aromatic ring.
21. How do you classify a primary, secondary or tertiary alcohol?
Answer: Count how many carbon groups are attached to the carbon bearing –OH: one = 1°, two = 2°, three = 3°.
Also remember: do not count the number of OH groups. Alcohol class predicts important chemistry: 1° and 2° alcohols give different oxidation products.
22. What do ortho/para- and meta-directing effects mean in aromatic substitution?
Answer: They describe which ring positions are favoured when a second substituent enters a substituted aromatic ring.
Also remember: electron-donating/activating groups usually direct ortho/para, whereas strong electron-withdrawing/deactivating groups usually direct meta.
23. Name the main carboxylic-acid derivatives by their group attached to the acyl carbon.
Answer: –Cl gives an acid chloride, –OR gives an ester, and –NR₂/–NH₂ gives an amide; an acid anhydride contains two acyl groups linked by oxygen.
Also remember: these derivatives undergo nucleophilic acyl substitution. Acid chlorides are especially reactive because chloride is a good leaving group.
24. What is the key difference between acid hydrolysis and basic hydrolysis of an ester?
Answer: Acid hydrolysis gives a carboxylic acid + alcohol; basic hydrolysis gives a carboxylate salt + alcohol.
Also remember: the basic process is saponification. To obtain the neutral carboxylic acid from the carboxylate, the mixture must be acidified.
25. What is the difference between R/S configuration and (+)/(−) optical rotation?
Answer: R/S describes absolute spatial configuration; (+)/(−) tells the experimentally observed direction of rotation of plane-polarized light.
Also remember: R does not automatically mean (+), and S does not automatically mean (−). A racemic 1:1 enantiomer mixture is optically inactive overall because the rotations cancel.
