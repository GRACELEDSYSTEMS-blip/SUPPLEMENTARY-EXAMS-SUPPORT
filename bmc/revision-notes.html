⚡ Last-Minute Mode

These are not bare arrows to memorize. Read the explanation under each pathway so the reaction makes sense.

Bonding decides shape.

Alkane carbon is sp³/tetrahedral; alkene carbon is sp²/trigonal planar; alkyne carbon is sp/linear.

π bonds increase reactivity.

The π bond is more exposed than a σ bond, so alkenes and alkynes commonly undergo addition reactions.

Restricted rotation matters.

C=C cannot freely rotate. Suitable alkenes can therefore exist as cis/trans geometrical isomers.

SN2 vs SN1.

SN2 is one-step and gives backside attack/inversion. SN1 is stepwise and forms a carbocation first.

E2 vs E1.

E2 removes β-H as the leaving group leaves in one step. E1 forms a carbocation first, then loses H⁺ to form C=C.

Carbonyl carbon is electrophilic.

Oxygen pulls electron density from C=O, leaving the carbonyl carbon partially positive and open to nucleophilic attack.

Lactone vs lactam.

Lactone = cyclic ester. Lactam = cyclic amide. Look for O versus N beside the carbonyl within the ring.

Amines are basic.

The nitrogen lone pair can accept H⁺. If the lone pair is made less available, basicity generally falls.

High-Yield Reaction Pathways — Understand These

1. Alkane → alkyl halide

Alkane + Cl₂/Br₂ —light/heat→ Alkyl halide + HX

This is free-radical substitution: a hydrogen on the alkane is replaced by a halogen. The carbon skeleton is not being oxidized into an alcohol yet; the important change is C–H → C–X.

2. Alkyl halide → alcohol

R–X + OH⁻ → R–OH + X⁻

This is nucleophilic substitution. Hydroxide acts as a nucleophile and replaces the halide leaving group, so the functional group changes from a haloalkane to an alcohol.

3. Alcohol ⇄ alkene

Alcohol —dehydration→ Alkene + H₂O

Dehydration removes H and OH as water and creates C=C. The reverse idea is hydration of an alkene, where H and OH are added across C=C to produce an alcohol.

4. Oxidation of alcohols

1° Alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid

A primary alcohol first oxidizes to an aldehyde. If oxidation continues, the aldehyde becomes a carboxylic acid. PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate) is useful when the aim is to stop at the aldehyde stage.

2° Alcohol → Ketone

A secondary alcohol oxidizes to a ketone. This is a major exam distinction: 1° alcohols and 2° alcohols do not give the same carbonyl product.

5. Reduction of carbonyl compounds

Aldehyde → 1° Alcohol   |   Ketone → 2° Alcohol

Reduction reverses the alcohol-to-carbonyl relationship: an aldehyde gives a primary alcohol, while a ketone gives a secondary alcohol. Recognize reducing agents such as NaBH₄ and LiAlH₄.

6. Esterification and hydrolysis

Carboxylic acid + Alcohol ⇌ Ester + H₂O

Esterification joins a carboxylic acid and an alcohol to form an ester and water. Acid hydrolysis is the reverse reaction and gives the carboxylic acid plus alcohol.

Ester + OH⁻ → Carboxylate salt + Alcohol

With strong base, ester hydrolysis gives a carboxylate salt, not the neutral acid. This base hydrolysis is called saponification.

TOPIC 1

Foundations + Alkanes

  • Alkanes: saturated hydrocarbons containing only C–C and C–H σ bonds; open-chain formula CnH2n+2.
  • sp³ carbon: tetrahedral, about 109.5°.
  • 1°, 2°, 3° carbon: carbon attached to one, two or three other carbons respectively.
  • Structural isomers: same molecular formula but different connectivity.
Physical trend: increasing chain length generally raises boiling point because London forces increase. Among isomeric alkanes, greater branching generally lowers boiling point because surface contact is reduced.

Combustion gives CO₂ + H₂O when complete. Cracking breaks larger hydrocarbons into smaller molecules. Reforming rearranges hydrocarbons and may form branched, cyclic or aromatic products.

TOPIC 2

Alkenes

Each C=C carbon is sp² hybridised and approximately trigonal planar. The double bond contains one σ bond and one π bond.

Hydrogenation

C=C + H₂ —Pt/Pd/Ni→ Alkane

Hydrogen adds across the double bond, removing the π bond and converting the unsaturated compound into a more saturated one.

Halogenation

C=C + X₂ → Vicinal dihalide

The halogen atoms add across C=C, one to each of the two alkene carbons.

Hydrohalogenation

C=C + HX → Alkyl halide

H and X add across the double bond. For an unsymmetrical alkene under the usual ionic conditions, know Markovnikov orientation: H tends to add so that the more stable carbocation pathway is followed, placing X on the more substituted carbon.

Hydration

C=C + H₂O/H⁺ → Alcohol

H and OH are added across C=C. This is the reaction relationship that connects an alkene back to an alcohol.

Oxidation

Cold alkaline potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) adds two OH groups across C=C to form a vicinal diol. Ozonolysis (O₃) instead cleaves the double bond to carbonyl-containing products.

Cis/trans check: each C=C carbon must have two different groups attached for the usual geometrical-isomerism situation.
TOPIC 3

Alkynes

The carbons of C≡C are sp hybridised and linear, about 180°.

Terminal alkynes have a relatively acidic terminal hydrogen. A sufficiently strong base can remove it to form an acetylide ion, which is useful as a carbon nucleophile.

Hydration

Alkyne + H₂O —H⁺/Hg²⁺→ Enol → Carbonyl compound

Water first adds to give an enol. The enol then rearranges by keto–enol tautomerism to the more stable carbonyl compound. Do not stop your answer at the enol when the final carbonyl product is required.

Also recognize addition of H₂, X₂ and HX across C≡C and the lecturer-listed Ag⁺ reaction of terminal alkynes.

TOPIC 4

Alkyl Halides + Alcohols

Substitution and elimination

SN2: one-step substitution. The nucleophile attacks as the leaving group departs; steric hindrance slows attack and backside attack gives inversion at a stereogenic reacting carbon.

SN1: stepwise substitution. The leaving group leaves first to form a carbocation, so carbocation stability matters.

E2: one-step elimination. A base removes β-H while the leaving group leaves and C=C forms simultaneously.

E1: stepwise elimination. A carbocation forms first, then loss of H⁺ gives the alkene.

Alcohol oxidation

1° alcohol + PCC → Aldehyde

PCC = pyridinium chlorochromate. It is useful when a primary alcohol should be oxidized to an aldehyde without deliberately continuing to the carboxylic acid.

1° alcohol + stronger/continued oxidation → Carboxylic acid

With stronger or continued oxidation, the aldehyde intermediate is further oxidized to the acid.

2° alcohol + oxidant → Ketone

A secondary alcohol gives a ketone because oxidation creates C=O at the OH-bearing carbon.

TOPIC 5

Stereochemistry

  • Chiral: not superimposable on its mirror image.
  • Enantiomers: non-superimposable mirror images.
  • Diastereomers: stereoisomers that are not mirror images.
  • Meso compound: contains stereocentres but is achiral because of internal symmetry.
  • R/S: absolute configuration assigned using priority rules.
  • Racemic mixture: equal amounts of two enantiomers; their opposite rotations cancel overall.
Why this matters biologically: receptors and enzymes are three-dimensional. Different stereoisomers can fit a biological target differently and therefore show different biological effects.
TOPIC 6

Aromatic Compounds

An aromatic system must be cyclic, planar, fully conjugated and contain 4n + 2 π electrons according to Hückel's rule.

Electrophilic aromatic substitution

The aromatic π system attacks an electrophile, temporarily forming a resonance-stabilized σ complex. A proton is then lost and aromaticity is restored.

Why substitution rather than ordinary addition? Substitution allows the ring to regain its aromatic stabilization; ordinary addition would destroy the aromatic π system.

For nitration, recognize the nitronium ion NO₂⁺ as the electrophile. For halogenation, a Lewis-acid catalyst helps generate the reactive halogen electrophile.

TOPIC 7

Aldehydes + Ketones

Aldehyde: R–CHO. Ketone: R–CO–R′. Oxygen pulls electron density from C=O, so the carbonyl carbon is electrophilic and can be attacked by nucleophiles.

Preparation of aldehydes

1° Alcohol + PCC → Aldehyde

PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate) oxidizes a primary alcohol to an aldehyde under conditions used to avoid deliberate over-oxidation to the acid.

Suitable alkene + O₃ → Carbonyl products

Ozonolysis cleaves C=C. The substituents originally attached to each double-bond carbon determine whether aldehydes and/or ketones are formed after the appropriate work-up.

Ester + DIBAH/DIBAL-H → Aldehyde

DIBAH = diisobutylaluminium hydride, also commonly written DIBAL-H. Under controlled partial-reduction conditions it can stop an ester at the aldehyde stage.

Oxidation, reduction and recognition

Aldehyde + [O] → Carboxylic acid

Aldehydes are readily oxidized. Tollens' reagent uses this difference: the aldehyde is oxidized while Ag⁺ is reduced to metallic silver, producing the familiar silver-mirror response.

Aldehyde + [H] → 1° alcohol   |   Ketone + [H] → 2° alcohol

Reduction adds hydrogen/reduces the carbonyl. Recognize sodium borohydride (NaBH₄) and lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH₄) as important reducing agents.

Why aldehydes are generally more reactive than ketones

Steric reason: an aldehyde has one carbon group and one H around the carbonyl carbon, whereas a ketone has two carbon groups, so nucleophilic approach is more crowded in the ketone.

Electronic reason: the two alkyl groups of a ketone donate electron density toward the carbonyl carbon more than the single alkyl group of an aldehyde, making the ketone carbonyl carbon less electrophilic.

Alcohol addition: hemiacetals and acetals

When an alcohol adds to a carbonyl, a hemiacetal can form: the same carbon bears both –OH and –OR. Further alcohol reaction under suitable acid conditions can replace the OH with another OR group to form an acetal.

Grignard reactions

A Grignard reagent, RMgX, behaves as a strong carbon nucleophile. Its R group attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon. After aqueous acidic work-up, the oxygen becomes –OH. The carbon from R is therefore added to the carbon skeleton.

HCHO + RMgX → 1° alcohol

Formaldehyde has no carbon substituent on the carbonyl carbon. Addition of one R group therefore gives a primary alcohol after work-up.

R′CHO + RMgX → 2° alcohol

An ordinary aldehyde already has one carbon group; Grignard adds another, so the OH-bearing carbon ends up attached to two carbons: a secondary alcohol.

R′COR″ + RMgX → 3° alcohol

A ketone already has two carbon groups; adding the Grignard R group gives three carbon groups around the OH-bearing carbon: a tertiary alcohol.

Ester + 2 RMgX → 3° alcohol

An ester undergoes two additions overall. The first addition leads to a ketone intermediate; that ketone reacts with a second equivalent of Grignard reagent. Acidic work-up gives a tertiary alcohol.

CO₂ + RMgX → Carboxylic acid after H₃O⁺ work-up

The Grignard carbon attacks carbon dioxide. After acidic work-up the product is a carboxylic acid containing one more carbon than the original Grignard carbon chain.

Fast Grignard pattern: formaldehyde → 1° alcohol; other aldehyde → 2° alcohol; ketone → 3° alcohol; ester + 2 equivalents → 3° alcohol; CO₂ → carboxylic acid.
TOPIC 8

Carboxylic Acids + Derivatives

The carboxyl group is –COOH. Loss of H⁺ gives a carboxylate ion; resonance spreads the negative charge over the two oxygens and stabilizes the conjugate base.

Strong hydrogen bonding contributes to relatively high boiling points. Short-chain acids are more water-soluble; solubility decreases as the non-polar hydrocarbon chain grows.

Esterification

Carboxylic acid + Alcohol ⇌ Ester + H₂O

The acid and alcohol combine to form an ester linkage. Because the reaction is reversible, water under acidic conditions can hydrolyse the ester back to the acid and alcohol.

Saponification

Ester + OH⁻ → Carboxylate salt + Alcohol

This is base hydrolysis of an ester. In the basic mixture the acid portion exists as its carboxylate salt; acidification would convert it to the neutral carboxylic acid.

Lactones and lactams

Lactone = cyclic ester. The ring contains –C(=O)–O–. Lactam = cyclic amide. The ring contains –C(=O)–N–.

Recognition shortcut: locate the carbonyl in the ring. If the atom next to that carbonyl within the ring is O, think lactone; if it is N, think lactam.

A γ-lactone/lactam commonly has a 5-membered ring, while a δ-lactone/lactam commonly has a 6-membered ring. Hydrolysis opens the ring: lactones give hydroxy-carboxylic-acid-type products, while lactams give amino-carboxylic-acid-type products under suitable conditions.

TOPIC 9

Amines

1° amine: one carbon group attached to N. 2°: two. 3°: three.

Amines are basic because the nitrogen lone pair can accept H⁺. When that lone pair is delocalized into an aromatic ring, it is less available to bind H⁺, so basicity can decrease.

Do not confuse amine with amide: in an amide, nitrogen is directly attached to a carbonyl carbon, C(=O)–N. A lactam is simply a cyclic amide.

Reaction Pathways — With the Meaning Included

This section is a quick recap of the connections already explained above.

Alkane → Alkyl halide → Alcohol → Alkene

Halogenation replaces H with X. Nucleophilic substitution can replace X with OH to make an alcohol. Dehydration of the alcohol removes water and creates C=C.

1° Alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid

Oxidation of a primary alcohol first creates an aldehyde. Further oxidation converts that aldehyde to a carboxylic acid. PCC is the key reagent to recognize when stopping at the aldehyde is desired.

2° Alcohol → Ketone

A secondary alcohol oxidizes to a ketone. This is why identifying the class of the starting alcohol is essential before predicting the oxidation product.

Aldehyde → 1° Alcohol   |   Ketone → 2° Alcohol

Reduction of C=O gives the corresponding alcohol. The number of carbon groups already attached to the carbonyl carbon determines whether the alcohol is primary or secondary.

Carboxylic acid + Alcohol ⇌ Ester + H₂O

Forward is esterification; the reverse under acidic aqueous conditions is ester hydrolysis.

Ester + OH⁻ → Carboxylate salt + Alcohol

This is saponification. Because the medium is basic, the carboxylic-acid portion remains deprotonated as a carboxylate until acidified.

Comprehensive Final Self-Check

Answer aloud before revealing the answer.

1. Why can suitable alkenes show cis/trans isomerism?
The π bond prevents free rotation around C=C, so fixed spatial arrangements can exist when each double-bond carbon has suitable different substituents.
2. What happens when a primary alcohol is oxidized, and why is PCC important?
A primary alcohol first gives an aldehyde and can be further oxidized to a carboxylic acid. PCC, pyridinium chlorochromate, is useful when the aldehyde is the desired oxidation product.
3. What distinguishes SN2 from SN1?
SN2 is concerted and one-step with no carbocation; SN1 is stepwise and forms a carbocation intermediate.
4. Why are aldehydes generally more reactive than ketones toward nucleophilic addition?
Aldehydes are less sterically crowded and their carbonyl carbon is generally more electrophilic. Ketones have two carbon groups that increase crowding and donate electron density.
5. What is a hemiacetal?
A compound in which the same carbon bears both an –OH group and an –OR group.
6. Give the Grignard product pattern for formaldehyde, aldehyde and ketone.
After acidic work-up: formaldehyde → primary alcohol; other aldehyde → secondary alcohol; ketone → tertiary alcohol.
7. Why does an ester require two equivalents of Grignard reagent?
The first addition produces a ketone intermediate, and that ketone undergoes a second Grignard addition. Acidic work-up then gives a tertiary alcohol.
8. What does RMgX + CO₂ give after acidic work-up?
A carboxylic acid containing one more carbon than the original Grignard carbon chain.
9. What is DIBAH and what transformation should you recognize?
DIBAH is diisobutylaluminium hydride, also written DIBAL-H. Under controlled partial-reduction conditions it can convert an ester to an aldehyde.
10. Distinguish a lactone from a lactam.
A lactone is a cyclic ester containing C(=O)–O in the ring. A lactam is a cyclic amide containing C(=O)–N in the ring.
11. What products result from base hydrolysis of an ester?
A carboxylate salt and an alcohol. This base hydrolysis is called saponification.
12. Why does benzene usually undergo substitution rather than ordinary addition?
Substitution allows the ring to regain or preserve aromatic stabilization; ordinary addition would destroy aromaticity.
13. What is the final product of acid/Hg²⁺ hydration of an alkyne?
An enol forms first, then undergoes keto–enol tautomerism to a carbonyl compound.
14. Why are amines basic?
The nitrogen lone pair can accept a proton.
15. Why can stereochemistry change biological activity?
Biological targets are three-dimensional, so different stereoisomers can fit and interact with a receptor or enzyme differently.
