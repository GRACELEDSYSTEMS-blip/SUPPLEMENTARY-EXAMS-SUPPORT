⚡ Last-Minute Mode

Do not memorize arrows without meaning. Understand what is changing and why.

Bonding decides shape.
  • Alkane carbon: sp³, tetrahedral.
  • Alkene carbon: sp², trigonal planar.
  • Alkyne carbon: sp, linear.
Unsaturation changes reactivity.

π bonds are more exposed than σ bonds, so alkenes and alkynes readily undergo addition reactions.

Restricted rotation matters.

A C=C bond cannot freely rotate. This is why suitable alkenes can exist as cis/trans isomers.

Understand alcohol oxidation.

Oxidation increases bonding to oxygen or decreases bonding to hydrogen. A 1° alcohol gives an aldehyde first; further oxidation gives a carboxylic acid. A 2° alcohol gives a ketone.

SN2 = one step.

Nucleophile attack and leaving-group departure happen together. Steric hindrance slows attack; inversion occurs at a stereogenic reacting carbon.

SN1 uses a carbocation.

The leaving group departs first, producing a carbocation. Carbocation stability therefore matters.

E2 = one step.

A base removes a β-H while the leaving group leaves and the C=C bond forms simultaneously.

Aromaticity gives special stability.

Benzene usually undergoes substitution because the ring can regain aromaticity.

Carbonyl carbon is electrophilic.

Oxygen pulls electron density away from carbon in C=O, leaving carbon partially positive and open to nucleophilic attack.

Reduction reverses the alcohol link.

Aldehyde → 1° alcohol; ketone → 2° alcohol.

Tollens points to an aldehyde.

An aldehyde is oxidized while Ag⁺ is reduced to metallic silver—the basis of the silver-mirror test.

Lactone vs lactam.

Lactone = cyclic ester. Lactam = cyclic amide. Look for O versus N next to the carbonyl within the ring.

Amines are basic.

The nitrogen lone pair can accept H⁺. If that lone pair is less available, basicity generally falls.

High-yield reaction chain

Alkane → Alkyl halide → Alcohol ⇄ Alkene
1° Alcohol → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid   |   2° Alcohol → Ketone
Aldehyde → 1° Alcohol   |   Ketone → 2° Alcohol
Carboxylic acid + Alcohol ⇌ Ester + H₂O
Ester + OH⁻ → Carboxylate salt + Alcohol
TOPIC 1

Foundations + Alkanes

  • Alkanes: saturated hydrocarbons containing only C–C and C–H σ bonds; open-chain formula CnH2n+2.
  • sp³ carbon: tetrahedral, approximately 109.5°.
  • 1°, 2°, 3° carbon: carbon attached to one, two or three other carbons respectively.
  • Structural isomers: same molecular formula, different connectivity.
Physical trends: increasing chain length generally raises boiling point because London forces increase. For isomeric alkanes, more branching generally lowers boiling point because the molecules have less surface contact.

Reactions to recognize

  • Combustion: complete combustion gives CO₂ + H₂O.
  • Halogenation: substitution of H by a halogen under radical conditions.
  • Cracking: breaks larger hydrocarbons into smaller molecules, often producing alkanes and alkenes.
  • Catalytic reforming: rearranges hydrocarbons to improve fuel quality and can form branched/cyclic/aromatic products.
TOPIC 2

Alkenes

Each C=C carbon is sp² hybridised and approximately trigonal planar. The double bond contains one σ bond and one π bond.

  • Hydrogenation: H₂/Pt, Pd or Ni adds across C=C → alkane.
  • Halogenation: X₂ adds across C=C.
  • Hydrohalogenation: HX adds across C=C; know Markovnikov orientation where applicable.
  • Hydration: H and OH add across C=C → alcohol.
  • Cold alkaline potassium permanganate (KMnO₄): gives a vicinal diol.
  • Ozonolysis: O₃ cleaves C=C to carbonyl products.
Cis/trans check: each double-bond carbon must have two different groups attached for the usual geometrical-isomerism situation.
TOPIC 3

Alkynes

The carbons of C≡C are sp hybridised and linear, about 180°.

  • Addition of H₂, X₂, HX and H₂O occurs across the triple bond.
  • Terminal alkynes possess a relatively acidic terminal H and can form acetylide ions under suitable strongly basic conditions.
  • Hydration under acidic Hg²⁺ conditions first gives an enol, which rearranges by keto–enol tautomerism to a carbonyl compound.
  • Recognize the Ag⁺ reaction used with terminal alkynes.
TOPIC 4

Alkyl Halides + Alcohols

Substitution and elimination

SN2: one-step substitution; nucleophile attacks as the leaving group departs. Steric hindrance is important and backside attack gives inversion at a stereogenic reacting carbon.

SN1: stepwise substitution; leaving group departs first to form a carbocation, then the nucleophile attacks.

E2: one-step elimination; base removes β-H while leaving group departs and C=C forms.

E1: stepwise elimination through a carbocation, followed by loss of a proton to form C=C.

Alcohol oxidation

1° alcohol + pyridinium chlorochromate (PCC) → aldehyde
1° alcohol + stronger/excess oxidant such as KMnO₄ or chromic acid → carboxylic acid
2° alcohol + oxidant → ketone

PCC means pyridinium chlorochromate. It is useful when the aim is to oxidize a primary alcohol and isolate the aldehyde rather than continue to the acid.

Reduction: aldehyde → 1° alcohol; ketone → 2° alcohol. Sodium borohydride (NaBH₄) and lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH₄) are important reducing agents to recognize.

TOPIC 5

Stereochemistry

  • Chiral molecule: not superimposable on its mirror image.
  • Enantiomers: non-superimposable mirror images.
  • Diastereomers: stereoisomers that are not mirror images.
  • Meso compound: contains stereocentres but is achiral because of internal symmetry.
  • R/S: absolute configuration assigned using priority rules.
  • Racemic mixture: equal amounts of two enantiomers; overall optical rotation cancels.
Biological importance: receptors and enzymes are three-dimensional, so two enantiomers may fit a biological target differently and show different activity.
TOPIC 6

Aromatic Compounds

An aromatic ring must be cyclic, planar, fully conjugated and contain 4n + 2 π electrons (Hückel's rule).

Electrophilic aromatic substitution: the ring attacks an electrophile → a resonance-stabilized σ complex forms → loss of H⁺ restores aromaticity.

  • Nitration: recognize formation/use of the nitronium electrophile, NO₂⁺.
  • Halogenation: halogen electrophile is generated with a Lewis-acid catalyst.
Core idea: substitution lets benzene preserve/regain aromatic stabilization; ordinary addition would destroy it.
TOPIC 7

Aldehydes + Ketones

Aldehyde: R–CHO. Ketone: R–CO–R′. The C=O bond is polar, so the carbonyl carbon is electrophilic and is attacked by nucleophiles.

Preparation of aldehydes

  • Primary alcohol + pyridinium chlorochromate (PCC) → aldehyde.
  • Suitable alkene + ozone (O₃) → carbonyl products. Ozonolysis cleaves C=C.
  • Ester + DIBAH/DIBAL-H → aldehyde under controlled partial-reduction conditions. DIBAH means diisobutylaluminium hydride; DIBAL-H is another common abbreviation for the same reagent.

Oxidation and reduction

Aldehyde + [O] → Carboxylic acid
Aldehyde + [H] → 1° alcohol   |   Ketone + [H] → 2° alcohol

Tollens' reagent: aldehydes are oxidized while Ag⁺ is reduced to metallic silver. Ordinary ketones are much more resistant to this oxidation.

Why aldehydes are generally more reactive than ketones

  • Less steric hindrance: an aldehyde has one carbon group and one H around the carbonyl carbon; a ketone has two carbon groups.
  • Greater electrophilicity: two alkyl groups in a ketone donate electron density and stabilize positive character at the carbonyl carbon more than the single alkyl group of an aldehyde.

Alcohol addition: hemiacetals and acetals

When alcohol adds to a carbonyl, a hemiacetal can form: the same carbon bears –OH and –OR. Further alcohol reaction under suitable acid conditions can replace the OH with another OR to form an acetal.

Grignard reactions — memorize the product pattern with understanding

A Grignard reagent (RMgX) behaves as a carbon nucleophile. The R group attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon. After aqueous acidic workup, the oxygen becomes –OH.

Formaldehyde (HCHO) + RMgX → 1° alcohol
Other aldehyde (R′CHO) + RMgX → 2° alcohol
Ketone (R′COR″) + RMgX → 3° alcohol
Ester + 2 equivalents RMgX → 3° alcohol
CO₂ + RMgX → carboxylic acid after acidic workup

For an ester, addition occurs twice overall: the first addition gives a ketone intermediate, which reacts again with Grignard reagent. Reaction with CO₂ adds one carbon to the Grignard carbon chain.

Fast memory rule: formaldehyde → 1° alcohol; aldehyde → 2° alcohol; ketone → 3° alcohol.
TOPIC 8

Carboxylic Acids + Derivatives

The carboxyl group is –COOH. Carboxylic acids are weak acids; loss of H⁺ gives a carboxylate ion, whose negative charge is resonance stabilized over two oxygens.

Strong hydrogen bonding contributes to their relatively high boiling points. Short-chain acids are more water-soluble; solubility falls as the non-polar hydrocarbon chain grows.

Carboxylic acid + Alcohol ⇌ Ester + H₂O
Ester + H₂O/H⁺ → Carboxylic acid + Alcohol
Ester + OH⁻ → Carboxylate salt + Alcohol

Saponification is base hydrolysis of an ester. Under basic conditions the acid portion remains as its carboxylate salt until acidified.

Lactones and lactams — exam recognition

Lactone = cyclic ester. The ring contains the ester linkage –C(=O)–O–.

Lactam = cyclic amide. The ring contains the amide linkage –C(=O)–N–.

How to distinguish them quickly: find the carbonyl carbon inside the ring. If the atom directly bonded to that carbonyl within the ring is O, think lactone; if it is N, think lactam.

Ring notation: γ- and δ-lactones/lactams correspond to rings formed when the reacting functional group is at the γ or δ position relative to the carboxyl carbon. A γ system commonly gives a 5-membered ring; a δ system commonly gives a 6-membered ring.

Hydrolysis: opening a lactone gives hydroxy-carboxylic-acid-type products; opening a lactam gives amino-carboxylic-acid-type products under suitable hydrolytic conditions.

TOPIC 9

Amines

1° amine: one carbon group on N. 2°: two. 3°: three.

Amines are basic because the nitrogen lone pair can accept H⁺. If that lone pair is delocalized into an aromatic ring, it is less available and basicity can fall.

Amide formation: an amine-derived nitrogen attached to a carbonyl carbon gives an amide functional group. Do not confuse an amine with an amide; in an amide the nitrogen is directly attached to C=O.

Comprehensive Final Self-Check

Answer aloud before revealing the answer.

1. Why can suitable alkenes show cis/trans isomerism?
The π bond prevents free rotation around C=C, so fixed spatial arrangements can exist when each double-bond carbon has suitable different substituents.
2. What is the oxidation pathway of a primary alcohol? What does PCC do?
1° alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid. PCC (pyridinium chlorochromate) can oxidize a primary alcohol to an aldehyde under conditions that allow the aldehyde to be isolated.
3. What distinguishes SN2 from SN1?
SN2 is concerted/one-step with no carbocation. SN1 is stepwise and proceeds through a carbocation.
4. Why are aldehydes generally more reactive than ketones toward nucleophilic addition?
Aldehydes are less sterically crowded and their carbonyl carbon is generally more electrophilic; ketones have two carbon groups that increase crowding and donate electron density.
5. What is a hemiacetal?
A compound in which the same carbon carries both an –OH group and an –OR group.
6. Give the Grignard product pattern for formaldehyde, aldehyde and ketone.
After acidic workup: formaldehyde → 1° alcohol; other aldehyde → 2° alcohol; ketone → 3° alcohol.
7. What is special about an ester reacting with a Grignard reagent?
Two equivalents add overall. A ketone intermediate forms and reacts again; acidic workup gives a tertiary alcohol.
8. What does RMgX + CO₂ give after acidic workup?
A carboxylic acid with one additional carbon relative to the Grignard carbon chain.
9. What is DIBAH and what transformation should you recognize?
DIBAH is diisobutylaluminium hydride (also commonly DIBAL-H). Under controlled conditions it can partially reduce an ester to an aldehyde.
10. Distinguish a lactone from a lactam.
A lactone is a cyclic ester and contains –C(=O)–O– in the ring. A lactam is a cyclic amide and contains –C(=O)–N– in the ring.
11. What products result from base hydrolysis of an ester?
A carboxylate salt and an alcohol.
12. Why does benzene usually undergo substitution rather than ordinary addition?
Substitution allows the ring to regain/preserve aromatic stabilization; ordinary addition would destroy aromaticity.
13. Why are amines basic?
The nitrogen lone pair can accept a proton.
14. Why can stereochemistry change biological activity?
Biological targets are three-dimensional, so different stereoisomers can fit and interact with a receptor or enzyme differently.
